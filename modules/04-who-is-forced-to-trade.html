MODULE 04 Why Price Moves

Who Is Forced To Trade

The fastest moves on your chart rarely come from someone with an opinion; they come from someone with no choice, and the useful question is never "who is right?" but "who has to trade here, and which way does their exit push?"

The Move No Headline Explains

You have seen it. No release, no headline, and price goes through your level as if it were not there, runs thirty points in four minutes, then stops dead and gives half of it back. Or the last hour of a quiet day suddenly moves more than the whole morning. You look for the reason, find none, and decide the market "knew something". The next time it happens, you chase it, or you fade it because it "makes no sense".

Most of those moves make perfect sense once you stop asking what someone thinks and start asking who had to transact. A dealer re-hedging an options book, a fund resetting its leverage before the close, a model selling because volatility rose, a cluster of stop orders turning into market orders one after another. None of them chose the price. All of them pushed it. This page teaches you to name them, in words, and to put what you learn in exactly one place on your plan.

The hierarchy this course uses everywhere is idea → setup (location + read) → trigger → construction (level → stop → risk → size → entry). Forced flow belongs to the idea. It can explain a move and it can weight your conviction. It is never a location, never a target, never a trigger, and never a direction. There are six parts, mechanical first: forced and voluntary liquidity, dealer hedging, the rebalancers and the calendar, systematic money, stop cascades and trapped traders, and last, the order you think in, and the pull of a story that explains everything.

Forced And Voluntary Liquidity

Every trade has two sides, but not every side is a decision. A fund manager who sells because she thinks the index is expensive can stop selling when price falls far enough. A holder who gets a margin call sells at whatever price is there, because the alternative is being closed out by the broker. The first is voluntary liquidity: it responds to price. The second is forced: it does not.

Forced Flow
Orders from a participant who must buy or sell regardless of price: a dealer re-hedging, a fund rebalancing to a rule, a margin call, a triggered stop. It changes how fast and how far price moves once it starts. It carries no information about where price should be.

The difference shows up in how price moves. Voluntary sellers thin out as price falls, so a move into them slows. Forced sellers don't thin out, so a move into them can speed up, and it often ends abruptly, when the forced trading is done rather than when price reaches anywhere in particular. In a balanced auction (Module 05) nobody is forced, which is why price floats there and moves stall.

Forced flow is real, and almost all of it is invisible to you in real time. You will rarely know its size. You can often know its shape: who would have to act, at what kind of event, and in which direction.

The rule: for each idea on your plan, name in words who would be forced to transact if price reached it, and which way their exit would push. If you can't name anyone, write "none known". That is the default, and it is the honest answer most days.

Getting it wrong looks like reading every move as a vote of opinion: fading a fast move because it "went too far", when the sellers in it could not stop at any price. What it reveals: you assume everyone on the other side can be reasoned with, so you stand in front of orders that cannot wait. The fix: the conviction reason on each branch of your plan (Module 15) carries one forced-flow clause, either a named participant and the direction of their exit, or the words "none known". A branch with neither clause is not finished.

Dealer Hedging, In Words

When you buy an option, someone sells it to you, and very often that someone is a dealer whose business is making markets, not taking a view. The dealer doesn't want to win or lose on direction, so they hedge: they hold futures (or stock) against the options book so that a small move in price leaves them roughly flat.

Dealer Hedging
The futures or stock a market maker holds against an options book so that small price moves leave it roughly flat. As price moves, the hedge the book needs changes, and the dealer must trade to keep up: forced flow that is set by the options book, not by any view on direction.

The catch is that the right hedge changes as price moves. How many futures an options book behaves like (options traders call this the book's delta; it is not the footprint delta of Module 07) shifts with every point. So the dealer must keep trading just to stay flat, and which way they trade depends on whether they are net long or net short options, a quantity called gamma.

  • Dealers long gamma (net long options): as price rises, their hedge leaves them too long, so they sell; as price falls, they buy. Their forced trading leans against the move. Ranges tend to compress; moves into levels tend to slow.
  • Dealers short gamma (net short options): as price rises, they must buy; as price falls, they must sell. Their forced trading goes with the move. Ranges tend to widen; breaks tend to run.
Gamma Regime (Long / Short Gamma)
Whether dealers, in aggregate, are estimated to be net long options (their hedging leans against moves) or net short (their hedging goes with moves). It describes how price may move, never where or which way. In this course it may weight conviction only; it is never a location, a target or an if-clause.

Two slower forces come from the same books. As time passes and as implied volatility changes, the hedge an options book needs drifts even when price stands still, and dealers trade to follow it.

Vanna / Charm
Vanna is how an option's hedge changes when implied volatility changes; charm is how it changes as time passes. Both make dealers trade with no move in price, and both build toward expiration.

Options that expire the same day (0DTE) carry the most of this in the least time, because an option's hedge swings hardest when it is near the money and close to expiry. That is part of why the open and the close of an expiration day can behave differently from the middle.

Now the limits, stated plainly. Nobody outside a dealer sees the dealer's book. Every published gamma figure is an estimate built from open interest and an assumed convention about who is long and who is short, and different vendors publish different numbers for the same day. And three questions are disputed among the people who study this, with no settled answer:

  • How big 0DTE effects are. One account says same-day options mostly dampen intraday moves; another says they can feed fast, one-way cascades on the days that matter. Both describe something real on some days.
  • The sign of vanna and charm. Sources disagree on which way dealers must trade when volatility falls, because the answer depends on who owns which options, which is the very thing nobody can see.
  • How large negative-gamma moves really are. Deeply negative estimates sometimes come with violent days and sometimes with ordinary ones. Short gamma can amplify a shock; it does not create one.

This page doesn't settle those disputes. Your plan doesn't need them settled, because of the rule below.

The rule: dealer and gamma levels are never locations, targets or if-clauses. No price from a gamma chart (a "flip", a "wall", a "key strike") goes on your level list, your target line or the "if" of any branch. The regime, if you use it at all, may weight conviction only, and it is written in one word: long, short or unknown. When you have no estimate, or price sits near where the estimate changes sign, the word is unknown, and it weights nothing.

Conviction
A label of strong, moderate or thin attached to one branch: how much structure and context stand behind its thesis. It is recorded for the journal. It doesn't change your size and it isn't a direction.

Getting it wrong looks like a "call wall" written on the level list, a branch that reads "if it loses the flip, short", or a target set at the "key gamma strike". What it reveals: a vendor's estimate of an invisible book has replaced the auction, and your stop now sits behind a number no one can verify. The fix: the regime word lives in one place only, the conviction reason, as "regime: long", "regime: short" or "regime: unknown". Before 09:25, read your level list, target line and every "if" clause against one test: did this price come from a gamma source? Any that did is struck through. Size never moves with the regime, because size never moves with conviction.

The Rebalancers And The Calendar

Some forced flow runs on a timetable. You can't see its size, but you can know its date and, often, its hour.

Leveraged ETFs. A leveraged fund promises a multiple of an index's return for one day: an issuer such as ProShares describes TQQQ as seeking three times the Nasdaq-100's daily performance. To keep that multiple, the fund has to reset its exposure every day; the SEC describes these funds rebalancing daily "in order to maintain a constant leverage ratio". The mechanism forces the direction of the trade. After an up day the fund's assets have grown and its exposure must grow with them, so it buys. After a down day it must cut, so it sells. Either way, it trades the same way the day already moved. Because the day's return is measured to the 4:00 pm ET net asset value, that reset is concentrated late in the session.

Leveraged ETF Rebalance
The daily trade a leveraged or inverse fund makes to restore its target multiple of an index's daily return. It buys after up days and sells after down days, in the direction the day already moved, and is concentrated toward the close.

Whether this flow moves prices much is itself disputed. The SEC, reviewing the research, found "some evidence, albeit inconclusive" that rebalancing affects prices, and noted that investor flows into and out of these funds can offset it. Desk commentary also argues over whether the last hour's volume comes mainly from funds pegging their trades to the closing price or from these leveraged products catching up. Both are forced. Neither is a direction you can trade.

Index rebalances. When an index changes its members or weights, the funds that track it must trade the change, and they do it at the close of the day before it takes effect. The changes are announced ahead, so dealers position early and the close is usually orderly despite its size. The Russell indexes, for example, are reconstituted twice a year from 2026, in June and December; FTSE Russell gives the December 2026 change as taking effect after the US close on December 11. That date belongs on your calendar as a close with heavy, forced activity, not as a call.

The rest of the calendar. Options expiration, month-end and quarter-end rebalancing, the quarterly roll (Module 03) and Tier One releases all bunch forced orders into known windows. A release is the sharpest of them: headline algorithms and triggered stops can send market orders through a level in seconds, which is why Module 02 has you flat five minutes before a Tier One release and out of new entries until two minutes after.

The rule: calendar events are activity context, never direction. They go on the calendar line of your pre-market sheet (Module 16) as notes about how much activity to expect, written in activity words only: "expiry Friday", "month-end close", "index rebalance close". No figure about how these days tend to move appears on the sheet. The close itself is handled by Module 17: nothing new from 15:55 ET, flat by 16:00.

Getting it wrong looks like "month-end, funds have to buy, so longs only today", or holding a position past 15:55 to "catch the rebalance". What it reveals: a timetable for activity has been turned into a forecast, and the forecast is now overriding the plan's levels and the session's close. The fix: the calendar line accepts only nouns: the event and its time. Any up, down, long, short or "should" written on it is struck through before 09:25. And the 15:55 alert from Module 17 is set on every day the calendar line names a close event, with no exceptions for "big" days.

Systematic Money That Trades On Thresholds

A large share of institutional money follows rules, not judgement, and the rules force trades whatever the price.

  • Vol-control funds hold more exposure when realized volatility is low and less when it is high. When volatility rises, they must sell, whatever their view; when it falls for long enough, they must buy back. Their selling comes after volatility has already risen, so it can add to a move that is underway.
  • CTAs (trend-following funds trading futures) buy and sell when price crosses thresholds in their models. The funds don't publish those thresholds. Bank desks publish estimates of them, and of how much might trade if a threshold breaks.
Vol-Control And CTA Flows
Rule-based fund trading forced by volatility or trend: vol-control funds cut exposure as realized volatility rises and add as it falls; CTAs buy and sell when price crosses thresholds in their trend models. Both trade after the conditions change, and outside estimates of their size and thresholds are guesses.

The useful idea is the feedback, not the number. A sharp, volatile drop can force vol-control selling and trend-model selling a day or two later, after the first move. A long, quiet climb lets both groups add steadily. Those are reasons to expect a move to carry or to stall. They are not places on a chart.

The rule: systematic flow is idea-level context. You may name it in an idea's conviction reason, in words ("volatility up three days; systematic selling possible"). A threshold price from a desk note is not a level and never goes on your level list, your target line or an if-clause. What does count as a level is Module 06.

Getting it wrong looks like reading "CTAs sell below 21,400" in a note, then shorting 21,400 with no level, read or trigger there. What it reveals: someone else's estimate of someone else's model has replaced your own location, and you can't say what would prove it wrong. The fix: a flow note you read enters your sheet as words only. Before 09:25, check every price on the sheet against the level list from Module 16; any price that came from a flow note instead is deleted.

Stop Cascades And Trapped Traders

The forced flow closest to your own chart is made of stop orders. Stops gather just beyond the obvious places: above a high that has held twice, below a low everyone can see. A stop is a market order waiting to happen. When price reaches the cluster, those orders fire, they push price further, and the push can reach the next cluster.

Stop Cascade
A fast move made of triggered stop orders: price reaches a cluster of stops, they execute as market orders, and their push carries price into the next cluster. It often ends abruptly, once the stops are used up, and the move can snap back.

The traders whose stops sit there are usually trapped traders. Balance builds positions on both sides. When price breaks out, the side that was wrong is forced out, and the move is built from their covering rather than from new buyers or sellers, which is why it can run hard and then fail to carry. When a breakout itself fails and price returns inside, the traders who bought the break are now the ones in the wrong place, with stops on the far side of the range.

Trapped Traders
Traders holding positions that price has just moved against, past the place they entered, such as buyers of a breakout that has fallen back inside the range. Their stops sit beyond the move, and their exit pushes price further against them. On a footprint chart this is the confirmation for the failed breakout and failed breakdown read (Modules 07 and 11).

Here is the second-order use. A trapped group tells you which way their exit pushes and roughly where it rests: below the range for trapped buyers, above it for trapped sellers. It doesn't tell you to trade. A sweep through a cluster on its own is not a trade in this course; a failed breakout or breakdown needs the reclaim and a trigger at the level (Modules 11 and 13). Order flow confirms; it never triggers.

Your own stop is in one of those clusters too. That is not a reason to move it. It is placed behind the far edge of a zone because that is where your idea is wrong, and a stop moved to "hide" from the crowd is a stop placed for a reason other than invalidation.

The rule: name where the trapped side's stops sit and which way their exit pushes, as context for the read. A cluster is not a location, not a target and not a trigger. Your own stop stays behind the far edge where you wrote it.

Getting it wrong looks like fading every sweep the moment it prints, or widening a stop "because they always hunt the obvious place". What it reveals: the sweep is being used as a trigger, and the stop is being treated as an enemy rather than as the price where your idea ends. The fix: before any order, read the trigger field you are about to record. If it would be blank, with only a sweep and no reclaim and trigger, there is no order. And widening a stop after entry is Broke Rule (Module 02); the journal tag is the count you review each week.

First, Second And Third Order, And The Story That Explains Everything

Most traders stop at the first question. The habit this page asks for is to keep going for two more.

First, Second And Third-Order Thinking
Three questions asked in order about any event or break. First order: what happened. Second order: who was already positioned for it. Third order: who is now forced to transact, and which way their exit pushes.
  1. First order: the event. A strong number, a break of the PD High, an expiration.
  2. Second order: who was already there. Crowded longs into good news, shorts who sold under a high all morning, buyers of the breakout.
  3. Third order: who must act now. The shorts whose stops sit above the high, the breakout buyers if price comes back inside, the dealer whose hedge just changed. And which way their exit pushes.

Good news into a market already full of buyers can produce selling: the second-order crowd has nobody left to sell to. A break with no headline can be nothing but the third order: stops, hedges and resets doing what they must.

The hard part isn't the ladder. It is what the ladder does to you. Flow stories explain every move after the fact, and explaining feels like knowing. "Dealers had to buy there." "The algos pushed it to the target." You will hear them all day, and each one makes the last move feel inevitable and the next one feel predictable. Neither is true. Most forced flow is invisible, estimates of it disagree, and any forced flow that becomes obvious gets traded ahead of until it stops working. A story about who must buy is also the most comfortable reason there is to hold a position the market has already invalidated.

The rule: the ladder is written before the session, in at most three short lines under an idea, and it feeds only that idea's conviction reason. It never changes a branch's direction, level, trigger, stop or target, and it is never a reason to exit. The four manual-flatten reasons in Module 02 are the only exits by hand.

Getting it wrong looks like rewriting the story every few minutes to fit each new bar, or staying in past your invalidation because "dealers have to defend this". What it reveals: the story has stopped describing the market and started protecting you from it. The fix: the three lines are written and dated before 09:25 and not edited during the session. Anything you think of after 09:25 goes in the post-session mark-up, where you check the lines against what happened. A manual exit whose only reason is a flow story is logged Too Early.

Forced flow tells you who has no choice and which way their exit pushes; it never tells you where to trade or which way price will go. It weights your conviction in one clause. Your level, read and trigger still make the trade.

Worked Case: One Trap At The PD High

Illustration · not a trade record

A composite MNQ morning, built to show the structure of a trap: who entered where, whose stops sit where, and which way their exit pushes. It belongs to no one, the prices are invented, and it stops before any trade: there is no trigger, no entry and no outcome. Prices are index points.

The sheet at 09:25. PD High zone 21,612 to 21,618 (price is below it, so the far edge is the top, 21,618). PD VAH zone 21,566 to 21,572. Open in value. Calendar line: "month-end close". No Tier One release today. Regime: unknown, since no estimate was used. Forced-flow clause on the PD High branch: "none known yet".

09:30 to 10:15: positions build. Price rotates between 21,570 and 21,610 and tests the PD High zone twice without a 5-minute close above it. Two groups form. Sellers short from 21,600 to 21,610 under the high, with buy-stops a few points above the zone, around 21,620 to 21,624. Buyers in the middle of the rotation, with sell-stops under the morning low, around 21,566.

10:22: first order, the break. Price trades through 21,618 to 21,631 in under two minutes. Second order: the shorts under the high were already positioned against it. Third order: their buy-stops at 21,620 to 21,624 fire as market orders and push price up through the cluster, joined by breakout buyers whose own buy-stop entries sit just above the high. The forced buying is spent by 21,631.

10:24 to 10:31: the break fails. Price makes no 5-minute close above 21,618; it prints two 1-minute closes back below 21,612, inside the zone's lower edge.

Who is trapped now. The breakout buyers from 21,620 to 21,631. Their stops sit below the zone, from about 21,605 down to 21,600, and a second cluster sits under the morning low at 21,566, where the earlier buyers' stops still rest. Which way their exit pushes: down. The covered shorts are flat and push nothing.

What goes on the plan. The forced-flow clause becomes: "breakout buyers trapped above the PD High; their exit pushes down, stops below 21,605 and 21,566". The regime stays unknown and weights nothing. The prices 21,605 and 21,566 are where forced orders rest; neither is a level, a target or an if-clause. The PD VAH zone at 21,566 to 21,572 is the level on the list in that area, and it is there for its own reasons.

That is where the case stops. The structure tells you which read is in play at the PD High (a failed breakout, Module 11) and which way the trapped side's exit pushes. Whether anything is ever entered depends on a reclaim and a trigger at a level (Modules 11 and 13) and a stop and size that clear construction (Module 14). None of that is shown here.

Notice what the case did not contain: no gamma level, no flow figure, no direction written before 10:31, and no trade. The same three questions were asked at each step, and the only thing that changed on the plan was one clause of conviction context.

Can You Name Who Is Forced?

Take your last five written plans, or today's sheet. Score one point for each line that is true for every idea on it.

  1. Who. The conviction reason names a forced participant and the direction of their exit, or says "none known".
  2. No flow prices. No price from a gamma chart, a flow note or a CTA estimate appears on the level list, the target line or any if-clause.
  3. Regime in one word. If a regime appears, it is long, short or unknown, inside the conviction reason and nowhere else.
  4. Calendar in nouns. The calendar line names events and times only, with no direction word.
  5. The ladder, before 09:25. Any first, second and third-order lines were written before 09:25 and not edited during the session.
  6. Stops where written. No stop was widened, or placed anywhere but behind a far edge, to avoid an "obvious" cluster.

6 of 6: forced flow is on your plan where it belongs. 4 or 5: fix the missing lines before your next session. Under 4: remove every forced-flow word from your plan and trade it without them until lines 1 to 4 pass on five plans in a row. One line overrides the score: if line 2 fails, treat the plan as under 4, whatever the total, because a price taken from someone else's estimate is now standing in for a level.

Before Your Next Session

Apply This Now

Under one idea on tomorrow's sheet, write three short lines before 09:25. First order: the event or the break at its level. Second order: who is already positioned there. Third order: who would be forced to trade, and which way their exit pushes. No prices from any flow source. That's the whole assignment.

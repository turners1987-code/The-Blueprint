MODULE 06 Why Price Moves

What Counts As A Level

A level is not a line price once reacted to; it is a place where a stop can sit behind something that already happened, and a line that cannot hold a stop is decoration, however often price has touched it.

The Line You Couldn't Put A Stop Behind

You know the moment. Price comes down into a line you drew, it pauses, and the trade looks right. Then you go to place the stop and there is nowhere to put it. Two ticks under the line feels too tight. Twenty points under feels arbitrary. You pick a number, the stop fills on a flicker, and price turns from the line without you. Or you skip the stop question, take the trade, and find out later that the line was a moving average, a round number, or a bounce from last Thursday that nobody else remembers.

The problem wasn't the entry. It was the line. This page gives you one test for what counts as a level, and then the two lists that pass it: the levels you mark before the session, and the ones that form during it. Every trade in this course traces one chain: idea → setup (location + read) → trigger → construction (level → stop → risk → size → entry). The level is where the setup lives and where construction starts. Get it wrong and nothing after it can be built.

There are seven parts, mechanical first: the stop-structure test, zones and the far edge, the pre-marked list, the in-session list, mid-range and the lines that are not levels, and locations against targets. The last part, the level you find after price has already turned, is about you rather than the chart.

The Stop-Structure Test

Stop-Structure Test
The test of whether a price counts as a level: you can name something that already happened there (a session extreme, a volume cluster, a value edge) and a price beyond it at which the reason for the trade is gone. A stop sits behind that price. A line that fails the test is not a level.

Show it with two lines on the same chart. Yesterday's RTH high is 24,186.50. Six five-minute bars traded up into it and turned; nothing traded above it. If you are short from that high and price trades through it and stays, the sellers who defended it are gone, and so is your reason for being short. A stop behind it has the high itself, and the bars that made it, standing between your entry and that stop. Now take a moving average sitting at 24,150. Nothing happened at 24,150. The line is there because of an arithmetic choice, and in ten minutes it will be somewhere else. Price through it tells you nothing about anyone's position. There is no price beyond it at which you are wrong, so there is no place for a stop.

The test doesn't ask whether price has reacted at a line, or how many times. Price reacts everywhere, in hindsight. It asks whether the structure gives your stop a job.

The rule: a price counts as a level only if you can write one sentence: "The stop goes behind [price] because [what happened there]." If you can't fill both blanks from the chart, it isn't a level.

Getting it wrong looks like a trade where the stop was chosen after the entry, as a number of points that "felt right". What it reveals: you chose where you wanted to trade, then looked for a reason, and the stop was left to cover the gap. It also fails the third check of the grade rubric in Module 14: the stop is behind structure, not inside noise. The fix: every row on your level list carries the stop sentence, written before the session. A row whose sentence has a blank is deleted before 09:25.

Zones And The Far Edge

Structure is never one price. A high is made by bars, a value edge sits next to rows of volume, a node has a top and a bottom. So every level is written as a zone.

Zone
A level written as a price band with two edges rather than a single line. Price can trade into a zone without breaking it; acceptance through the whole zone is what changes the read.
Far Edge
The edge of a zone on the far side from where price arrives. For a level above current price it is the top of the zone; for a level below, the bottom. The stop goes behind it.

Show it on the same high. The bar that printed 24,186.50 opened at 24,178.00 and closed at 24,180.25. One way to build that zone runs from the top of the bar's body, 24,180.25, to the high itself: 24,180.25 to 24,186.50, six and a quarter points. Price below it arrives from underneath, so the near edge is 24,180.25, where the defending side first showed up, and the far edge is 24,186.50. Price trading inside those six points is price testing the level, not the level failing. Price accepted above 24,186.50 is the level gone.

Two consequences follow, and both belong to later modules. The zone's width is part of your risk: a fill at the near edge needs the whole zone, plus whatever you put behind the far edge, before the stop is reached. And where inside the zone you get filled decides how far that stop is; drift a few points past the near edge and the same stop gets further away. Module 14 turns both into the stop and the size.

How you build each type of zone is a construction you choose once and keep: the body-to-extreme rule above for a high or low, the widest rows around a POC, the edge row and the first thin row beyond it for a value edge, the outer rows of a node. The point isn't which construction. It is that the same slug is built the same way every session, so a level that failed can be told apart from a level drawn somewhere else.

The rule: every level is a zone with a near edge and a far edge, built by a construction written once per slug. Where two zones overlap, they become one zone that names every slug in it.

Getting it wrong comes in two forms. A single-price line with the stop two ticks beyond it, stopped out by a probe that never threatened the structure. Or a zone redrawn wider after price traded through it, so the trade "is still fine". What it reveals: in the first, the level was treated as a wire, and the stop sat inside the noise around it. In the second, the zone is being drawn to fit the trade you are in. The fix: one construction line per slug at the top of your level list ("pd-high: extreme to the nearer body edge of the 5-minute bar that printed it"). Edges are written by 09:25 and never moved during the session. Module 17 puts two alerts on every zone, one at each edge.

The Pre-Marked List And The 16:00 Boundary

Pre-Marked Level
A level built before the session from completed data and taken from one fixed list: the prior day, settlement, the overnight session, the prior week and the higher-timeframe profile. Each carries a session scope, and only these go on the pre-market sheet.

The pre-marked list is fixed. Each slug passes the stop-structure test because each is something that already happened in a session that has finished. Each one also has a session scope: the bars that build it.

Session Scope
The stretch of bars that builds a level. Prior-day levels and settlement: RTH, 09:30 to 16:00 ET. Prior-week levels: the full-session week, Sunday 18:00 to Friday 16:00 ET. Overnight levels: ETH, from 16:00 ET to the 09:30 ET open.

The 16:00 ET boundary from Module 03 is where this list gets its edges. A high printed at 16:40 ET is not the prior day's high, however close to the close it was: it is an ETH bar, so it belongs to the overnight range. A platform template that runs to 17:00 ET, or that uses CME's trading day, builds a different prior day from different bars, and its pd-high can be a different price. Neither is wrong. They are different definitions, and you mark from one. Data sites that publish level statistics may use other boundaries too, so their levels can differ slightly from yours.

The htf rows carry dates because composite nodes age. A node built from sessions two months ago, before a large move, may describe an auction nobody is positioned in any more. How long a node stays useful is something your own record can show you, if every use of one carries the composite's dates.

The rule: only these slugs go on the pre-marked list. Each row carries its slug, its zone, its far edge and its session scope, and is built from chart templates that end at 16:00 ET.

Getting it wrong looks like a pd-high taken from a full-session chart on Monday and an RTH chart on Tuesday, or an overnight high that was really printed at 16:30. What it reveals: the levels came from whatever the screen happened to show, not from a definition, so when a level fails you can't tell whether the market or the drawing was at fault. The fix: keep two saved templates, RTH and full session, both ending at 16:00 ET, and build each slug only from the one its scope names. Write the scope in brackets on each row: "pd-high 24,180.25 to 24,186.50 (RTH)".

The In-Session List

In-Session Level
A level that forms during the current session, from one of the types on the in-session list. It counts only once it has finished forming, and only if your plan named its type before 09:25.

Some structure doesn't exist at 09:25. It is built in front of you, and the list of what counts is just as fixed as the pre-marked one. Each type has a moment at which it becomes a level. Before that moment it is a moving price, and a moving price fails the stop-structure test because the thing behind the stop hasn't finished happening.

Opening Range (OR)
The high and low of the session from 09:30 ET to the end of a window you choose and write on your plan. They become levels, or-high and or-low, only once the window has closed.
Defended Pullback High / Low
In the current session, the low of a pullback in a move up (or the high of a pullback in a move down) after which price went on beyond the swing extreme that came before the pullback. It stays a level until price trades back through it.
Developing VPOC / VAH / VAL
The current session's VPOC and value edges as its profile builds. They move as volume builds, so each is used at the price it shows when your trigger fires, and that price is the one you record.

The last type is read from the footprint chart. A stacked imbalance is a cluster of adjacent prices on the 5-minute footprint where aggressive volume ran heavily one way, with delta running the same way; Module 07 defines it and its settings, whose working default is ratio 5 and minimum delta 15, chosen for screen clarity. On this page it is a location only, never a trigger.

SlugsIt counts fromThe structure behind the stop
or-high · or-lowThe close of the window you wroteThe session's opening extreme
defended-pullback-high · defended-pullback-lowThe bar that takes out the earlier swing extremeThe bars that made the pullback's turn
developing-vpoc · developing-vah · developing-valThe moment your trigger fires there; the price at that moment is recordedToday's volume around it
unfinished-businessThe prior session's close, or, for one made today, the second 30-minute period to touch a flat extreme with nothing beyond itThe flat extreme itself; mostly a target
stacked-imbalanceThe close of the 5-minute bar that completes the stackThe stack's prices, top to bottom

Three notes on the list. The OR window length is yours to set: whatever you choose, write it once and keep it, because an opening range whose window changes from day to day isn't one definition. Unfinished Business is the poor high or poor low from Module 05; it sits on the in-session list because its status is settled during the session, and the moment price trades through it, the business is done and the line is gone. And a stacked imbalance is read from the footprint, which Module 07 teaches; testing any footprint location over many years needs historical tick data with the aggressor side, which a platform's own short tick history doesn't hold. That is a fact about testing footprint reads, and it is why your own tagged record of each stack matters.

The rule: an in-session level counts only when it has finished forming by its own condition in the table, and only if your plan named its type before 09:25. Every trade at one is tagged with its slug.

Getting it wrong looks like an "OR high" used at 09:38 when the window you wrote runs to 09:45, or a developing VPOC chased as it slides with price. What it reveals: a moving price was treated as a finished one, so the stop had nothing finished behind it. The fix: the plan carries one in-session line naming the types you will watch and each one's ready condition: "or-high / or-low after 09:45; stacked-imbalance on the 5-minute close". A type not on that line doesn't count that session, however good it looks. Price at an unlisted spot gets a note for the mark-up, not an order (Module 17).

Mid-Range, And The Lines That Are Not Levels

Mid-Range
The location tag for any price outside every zone on your list: no named level, so nothing for a stop to sit behind. It exists so that a trade taken there is counted rather than hidden.

Mid-range is not a kind of level. It is the absence of one, and it gets a name for one reason: so it can be counted. Most of a chart, most of the time, is mid-range. Price sitting there at 09:10 is normal and asks nothing of you.

Then there are lines that look like levels and fail the test. In this framework VWAP, pivots, moving averages, Fibonacci levels, round numbers, fair value gaps and chart patterns such as trendlines and triangles are not locations. The reason is the same for each. A moving average or VWAP is an average that moves with price, so it never marks a place where something happened. A pivot or a Fibonacci line is arithmetic on someone else's choice of two prices. A round number is a property of the decimal system. A fair value gap is a stretch of candles that didn't overlap, with no volume record of anyone defending it. A trendline can be drawn a dozen ways through the same bars. None of them gives you a price beyond which a position is plainly gone. The list doesn't grow to include them.

That doesn't make them invisible. Where one of these lines happens to sit inside a real zone, the zone is the level and the line adds nothing to it. Where it sits outside every zone, the price is mid-range, whatever is drawn there.

The rule: a price outside every zone on your list is mid-range. A trade there is tagged mid-range, and it fails the first check of the grade rubric: the location is a named level.

Getting it wrong looks like a trigger taken between two zones, with the stop "about twenty points" away because nothing was there to put it behind, usually in the first minutes of the session. What it reveals: the archetype of the trader with a trigger and no location. The trigger fired, so the trade felt planned, but movement was being traded instead of a place. The fix: before any order, write the location slug in the journal's location field. If the only honest entry is mid-range, write that, and no order goes in. Your weekly count of mid-range entries is the measure of this item.

Locations And Targets

Location
Where a trade happens: one named level from the pre-marked or in-session list, written as a zone, with the stop behind its far edge. It is recorded by its slug in the journal's location field.
Target
A level a trade aims at, written before entry. It says where price may travel to; it is never a reason to enter and never a direction.

The same level can be either. The pd-vpoc zone is a location when you enter there with your stop behind its far edge. It is a target when you entered somewhere else and it is where the trade is aimed. The level didn't change. Its job in the trade did.

Some levels on the list do one job far more often than the other. Unfinished Business is mostly a target: a poor high is where a move already underway can reach, not a reason to start one. A pd-vpoc in reach of an open in prior value is a target first. Treating a target as a location is how a trade gets entered from the middle of nowhere "because price has to go there".

The rule: every level on your list is written in one of two columns, location or target, for that session, and a level in the target column is never the reason for an entry. Every trade at a level also records the test number: first touch of the zone that session, second, and so on.

The figure behind the pd-vpoc line is a target figure only. In TradingStats' NQ data, on sessions that opened inside the prior session's value area, the prior session's VPOC was touched 82.4% of the time (TradingStats, app data, n=994). That is a touch rate measured from the open. It says nothing about which way price went first, when the touch came, or where an entry would have sat, and a well-visited target still loses money from an entry in the wrong place. It tells you where to aim. It never tells you to enter.

The test number is there because whether a second test of a level behaves like the first is a question your own record answers, and it can only answer it if every trade carries the number.

Getting it wrong looks like "opened in value, so the VPOC has to get hit", followed by a long from mid-range toward it. What it reveals: a probability about a destination was used as a reason to start the journey, and the location, the read and the trigger were skipped. The fix: your level list has a target column and a location column, and a branch on your plan may name a target-column level only after the word "then". If the only level supporting an entry is in the target column, the entry has no location and is not taken.

The Level You Find Afterwards

Look at any chart after the close and levels are everywhere. Price turned at a line, so the line must have been a level. It is the most convincing illusion on the screen, because hindsight hands you the turn first and the reason second. Every price that turned looks like it meant something, and every price that didn't is forgotten.

It works on you during the session too. A line appears at 10:20 that wasn't on the plan, because you want a trade and the chart has gone quiet. It looks like a level. It has a story. And you can't tell, at that moment, whether you found it or wanted it.

This is the hard part, because a level you draw yourself feels like insight. The protection isn't a better eye. It is a list that was closed before you needed anything from it.

The rule: the pre-marked list is closed at 09:25, and the in-session list adds only the types named on the plan, by their own ready conditions. Nothing else becomes a level during the session.

Getting it wrong looks like a trade at a line drawn at 10:20, or a post-session review that "finds" the level a losing trade should have used. What it reveals: the need to be in a trade, or to have been right, is drawing the chart for you, and a chart drawn by wanting will always agree with you. The fix: at the end-of-day mark-up, count the lines you drew or noticed after 09:25 and write the count in the journal. Any trade at one is tagged mid-range. If you think a new type of structure deserves a place on the list, it goes into your register as an idea to test, never onto tomorrow's chart.

A level is a zone of finished structure with a far edge a stop can sit behind, taken from a fixed list. Everything else is mid-range, however it looks, and a level that price is expected to reach is a target, never a reason to enter.

08:50. Templates first. RTH template, 09:30 to 16:00 ET; full-session template, ending 16:00 ET. Nothing is drawn yet.

09:00. The data, by scope. From Tuesday's RTH bars: high 24,186.50 (touched in two separate 30-minute periods, nothing above: a poor high), low 24,032.00 (a tail of thin prints under it: excess), VAH 24,148.00, VAL 24,071.00, VPOC 24,112.00, all from the volume profile. Settlement: 24,131.50. From the ETH bars, 16:00 ET Tuesday to now: high 24,190.00, printed at 16:12; low 24,094.50; POC 24,115.00. From the prior full-session week: high 24,262.00, low 23,958.00. From a 20-session composite (dates written on the row): an HVN at 24,205.00 to 24,222.00 and an LVN at 24,010.00 to 24,022.00.

The 16:12 print is where the boundary does its work. It sits above Tuesday's RTH high, but it came after 16:00 ET, so it belongs to the overnight range: the on-high is 24,190.00 and the pd-high stays 24,186.50. A template that ran to 17:00 ET would have put that print in Tuesday and shown a pd-high of 24,190.00. And a chart zoomed to the hours after midnight shows an overnight high of 24,166.00, which misses everything before midnight, including the 16:12 print. Both are checked before anything is drawn.

The pd-vpoc and on-poc zones overlap the same way. Each row also carries its stop sentence; the top row's reads: "The stop goes behind 24,190.00 because the prior session's high and the overnight high both sit inside the zone."

09:15. Mid-range. Every price outside those eleven rows. Price sits in it now, between settlement and pd-vah, 16.50 points of nothing. Inside the prior day's range the widest stretches are 24,037.50 to 24,066.00 (28.50 points between pd-low and pd-val) and 24,153.00 to 24,180.25 (27.25 points between pd-vah and the top zone). Nothing is due there.

09:18. Lines considered and not marked. The overnight session's VWAP at 24,126.00: an average that moves with price; it sits in mid-range just above the pd-vpoc zone, so it is not written. The round number 24,100.00: 1.00 point above the on-low zone and inside nothing, so it is not written. A Fibonacci line from last week's swing and a trendline across three overnight lows: neither has a price beyond it at which a position is gone. Struck, all four.

09:20. The in-session line. "Watching: or-high / or-low after 09:45; defended-pullback-high / low on the bar that takes out the earlier swing; developing-vpoc / vah / val at the trigger price; stacked-imbalance on the 5-minute close, ratio 5, minimum delta 15." The 15-minute OR window is this composite list's own choice, written once; it is not a setting this page recommends.

09:22. Targets. Tuesday's poor high at 24,186.50 would have been unfinished-business, but the 16:12 ETH print at 24,190.00 traded through it, so the business is done and it does not go in the target column. The pd-low had excess, so it isn't Unfinished Business either. In the target column: if the open is in prior value (between 24,071.00 and 24,148.00), the pd-vpoc and on-poc zone as the first target in play. It is not a reason to enter.

09:25. The list closes. Eleven rows, one in-session line, one target column, the mid-range written in. The case stops here. Which of these zones a trade comes from, with what read and what trigger, is the plan's job, and what happens after 09:30 belongs to Module 17.

Notice what the case spent its time on. Not on finding more lines: it struck four. Most of the work was scope (which bars build which level, and the 16:12 print that set the on-high and settled the poor high) and edges. The list that came out is long because the structure was dense. On a different morning the same method gives five rows, and that is fine.

Does Your Chart Pass?

Take the chart you marked for your last session, or mark one now. Score one point for each line that is true.

  1. Slugs. Every pre-marked row is a slug from the list on this page, and nothing else is on the chart as a level.
  2. Zones. Every row has a near edge, a far edge and its side of price.
  3. Stop sentence. Every row has its "the stop goes behind [price] because [what happened there]" sentence, with both blanks filled.
  4. Scope. Every row names its session scope, and the RTH levels came from bars that end at 16:00 ET.
  5. Construction. Each slug was built by a construction you have written down and used the same way the session before.
  6. In-session line. The plan names the in-session types you will watch, each with its ready condition, and the OR window if you use one.
  7. Targets apart. Target-column levels are marked as targets, and none of them is the only support for an entry.

7 of 7: the chart is ready to plan from. 5 or 6: write the missing lines before 09:25; until they are written, the rows they affect carry no branch. Under 5: no trade at a level that session; mark the chart again tomorrow from blank. Two lines override the score. A row that fails line 2 or line 3 has no stop placement, so that row is struck for the day, whatever the total.

Before Your Next Session

Apply This Now

Before tomorrow's open, take one level from your chart and write it as one row: slug, near edge, far edge, side of price, session scope, and the stop sentence. One row. That's the whole assignment.

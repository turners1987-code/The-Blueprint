MODULE 20 Process

The Trader's Mind

Your mind is not something to master before you trade: it is the thing every hard rule was already built around, and each mistake it makes is fixed by a mechanism, never by willpower.

Copies this lesson as Markdown to paste into any AI assistant

Right About The Market, Wrong About The Morning

You marked the level. You wrote the idea. Price did what you said it would. And the session still ended worse than the read deserved, because somewhere between 09:30 and 11:00 the trades stopped coming from the plan and started coming from you: the second entry taken because the first one was easy, the stop-out answered forty seconds later, the exit taken because a red candle looked big.

That gap, between a right read and the trades actually placed, is what this page is about. It is not fixed by being more disciplined. Discipline is what a working set of mechanisms looks like from the outside, not something you bring to the screen and spend. So every mistake below gets three things: what it looks like, what it reveals about you, and a mechanism that fixes it: a setting, a written line, a rehearsed routine or a field in the journal.

There are seven parts. The first two are mechanical: where each hard rule came from, and breaks. The last two are the hardest: curious versus needing, and the one failure this course recognises.

Where Each Hard Rule Came From

The five rules in Module 02 look like restrictions. Each one is the written answer to a specific behaviour, a thing traders do to themselves on a bad day often enough that it now has a rule in front of it. When a rule feels arbitrary in the moment, the behaviour it stops is the part to remember.

Broke Rule
The journal tag for breaking a hard rule: an entry outside the trading window or from 15:55 ET, a position held through a Tier One release or still open at T−5, a stop widened after entry, a new entry with no post-loss reset, or trading past the kill switch.

None of these rules predicts anything about price. Each one moves a decision from the moment you are least able to make it to the moment you are most able to: before the session.

The rule: keep the rules as written, and when one feels like a cost, read the behaviour it stops, not the rule.

Getting it wrong looks like bargaining: "the window is for normal days", "the reset is for big losses", "the news rule is for people who don't know the levels". What it reveals: you are reading the rule as a price you pay rather than as a decision you already made, so every session reopens it. The fix: copy the middle column, in your own words, onto the card beside your screen, one line per rule, and read it aloud before the first order of the day. A rule you can explain is harder to bargain with than a rule you can only recite.

Scheduled Breaks Are Hygiene

Scheduled Break
A break from the screen, written on the plan before the session at a fixed time, and taken whether the session is going well or badly. It is never triggered by a result.

Attention drains with screen time. A session that runs from the open to the close without a gap asks for the same quality of decision at 14:30 as at 09:45, and you know it does not get it. A break taken at a fixed time is the cheapest repair there is: the platform is closed, you are away from the desk, and you come back to the plan rather than to the last trade.

Two things keep a break honest. First, it is on the plan before the session, so it is not a reward for a good morning or a punishment for a bad one. A pause after a loss is a different thing with a different name: the Post-Loss Reset, below. Second, it carries no promise. A break, a day away or a holiday is hygiene, like sleep. It is not a cure, nothing on this site says any cadence of breaks improves results, and the same habits come back with you when you return. That is why returning has its own protocol.

Coming back from time away. After any day or more away from the screen, the first session back starts with the full pre-market routine from scratch (Module 16), not a glance at yesterday's levels, and with one rehearsal of the reset before the first live order. Feel takes time to come back. The routine doesn't need it to.

The rule: breaks are written on the plan at fixed times and taken regardless of the result; after time away, the full routine runs before the first order.

Getting it wrong looks like skipping the 11:00 break because the morning is green, taking an unplanned one only after a loss, or coming back from a week off and placing an order at 09:31. What it reveals: breaks are being run by your P&L, so the moments you most need one are the moments you will skip it. The fix: write the break times on every plan, set an alarm for each, and close the platform when it rings, not minimise it. After time away, the first line of the plan reads "return day: full routine, reset rehearsed", and it is ticked before the first order. Module 17 covers what happens between the breaks: alerts at your levels instead of eyes on every candle.

The Post-Loss Reset Is A Rehearsed Skill

Post-Loss Reset
The fixed routine run after every losing trade and before any new entry: cancel every working entry order, take a physical step to slow down, then answer three questions aloud. If you can't answer them clearly, you are not ready to trade.

The reset is set out in Module 02: cancel every working entry order; feet flat, one long exhale, three or four rounds of box breathing; then three questions answered aloud. This section is about the part Module 02 can only state: a reset is a skill, and a skill is built by repetition in the conditions it is for.

A routine you have only ever run while calm is a routine you have never run at all, as far as the moment after a stop-out is concerned. That moment comes with a raised pulse, a narrowed view and a hand already moving toward the order ticket. If the first time you run the reset is the morning you need it, it will be skipped, shortened or run with your eyes on the chart.

The sequence, in the order it runs.

  1. Cancel every working entry order. Any resting stop on a position you still hold stays; entry orders go. The first impulse after a loss is a re-entry, and a working order is that impulse already placed.
  2. The physical step. Feet flat, one long exhale, three or four rounds of box breathing.
  3. The three questions, aloud, in sentences. What's the chart telling me? Are any setups forming? What would need to happen for a setup to present itself?
  4. The third answer, written. One line in the journal, before any new entry. A blank line means no trade.

If you cannot answer clearly, you are not ready, and the next trade waits. Waiting means away from the order ticket: stand up, leave the desk, come back, and run the sequence again from step 2.

Rehearsing it. Once a week, with no position on, run the whole sequence with your heart rate up: straight after a flight of stairs or a fast walk, or straight after a losing trade in the simulator. You are practising the routine in a body that resembles the one you will be in when it matters. Count the rehearsals in the journal the way you count trades.

The rule: after every loss, the full reset before any new entry, and the reset rehearsed weekly under raised arousal.

Getting it wrong looks like a reset run at the screen with the chart still moving, or skipped because "it was only a small loss". What it reveals: the reset is being treated as a mood to wait out, not a routine to run, and it fails exactly when the loss is the one that matters. The fix: the written third answer, timestamped before the next entry, is the evidence the reset happened; your weekly review checks that every post-loss entry has one. The rehearsal count sits next to it. A reset with no rehearsals behind it is a plan, not a skill.

The Re-entry Loop After A First Win

Module 02 names the re-entry loop: a run of entries taken one after another without a fresh setup, usually starting after the session's first win. It is worth seeing why it starts with a win and not a loss.

The first trade wins. It felt easy, and easy feels like information. The next entry comes a little faster, from a little further away from a level, and with a trigger you are a little more willing to see. Nothing about the market has told you the second trade is better; the first trade's result has. When one of these loses, the trade after it is taken to get it back, and the session has stopped being a series of setups and become a series of answers to the last trade. The one whose best mornings are followed by their worst afternoons is running this loop.

There is no cap on the number of trades in this course, and no rule that stops you after a win or after giving some of it back. The 2R kill switch caps the loss for the day. It does not protect a win, and nothing here pretends to. What protects the morning is that the second trade is held to exactly the same test as the first.

Grade (A+ / A / B / C)
The entry-plan score given to every trade at entry, before it resolves, from four checks: the location is a named level, the trigger fired before entry, the stop is behind structure, and the size matches the plan for that stop. A+ is all four with a fill within 2 ticks of the intended price; A is all four; B is three of four with no breach of the stop or size check; C is two or fewer, or any breach of those two. C is no trade.

The rule: every entry after the first, and especially the first after a win, gets the four checks written before the order goes in. A C is not taken.

Getting it wrong looks like a second entry from the middle of the range, inside two minutes of the first trade closing, with the grade filled in afterwards. What it reveals: the first win was read as a forecast of the second, and the grade had become a description of what you did instead of a test you applied. The fix: the grade line is written before the order: level, trigger, stop, size, grade. In the weekly review (Module 18), sort each session's trades by their position after the first win and read the grades down the column. The journal shows your trade count per session as data; a loop shows up there as a cluster.

Losing Is A Member Of A Distribution

If your read is right six times in ten, the four are not mistakes. They are the other four. A trade built to plan, at a named level, with a trigger, a structural stop and size from that stop, that hits its stop has done exactly what the plan said it might.

What surprises people is how those four arrive. They do not space themselves out. Even when every trade is independent of the last, losses arrive in runs: over a few dozen trades, a run of three in a row is the ordinary case, not the rare one. A streak of losses is what a plan followed to the letter looks like a good part of the time.

So two errors sit on either side of a losing run. The first is reading it as a verdict and changing the plan after three trades. The second is reading it as nothing when it is something: three losses that each carried a Broke Rule tag are not a distribution, they are a habit. The journal separates the two, because a correctly built plan trade that lost gets no negative tag.

Negative Tag
A journal tag that records an emotion-driven or rule-breaking action on a trade, split into entry and exit: for example Impulsive, Too Early or Broke Rule. A trade built and exited to plan that lost carries none.

Judgement about a setup waits for a sample. In the journal's analysis, any cohort below 30 trades, or below 20 distinct sessions, is shown dimmed with a warning, because thirty trades from four sessions is one week's mood, not a sample. Below that floor, the only review allowed is a rule-break audit: were the losses built to plan, or not? And reviews run on a fixed schedule, never because a run of losses called for one.

The rule: a plan trade that lost gets no negative tag and no review of its own; setups are judged in cohorts at the display floor, on the review schedule, never after a losing run.

Getting it wrong looks like an A trade that lost tagged "bad entry", a setup dropped after three red days, or a midweek review called because Tuesday hurt. What it reveals: you are grading outcomes, so your journal is learning the market's noise instead of your behaviour. The fix: tag at the exit, from the four checks and the four flatten reasons only, never from the R column. Put the weekly review in your calendar for the same time every week; a losing day changes nothing about when it happens.

Curious Or Needing

There are two ways to sit down in front of a chart. Curious: you want to see what the market does at your levels, and you would be content to watch it do something you cannot trade. Needing: you want something from the session, whether it is a green day, a loss repaid, being right about last night's idea, or simply something to happen. The market is the same either way. What you see in it is not.

Need is the condition under which the earlier sections fail. A trader who needs the day to work hears a setup in every bar, takes the second trade because the first was not enough, and finds the reset intolerable because it delays the answer. Boredom is a quiet form of need: a slow market manufactures trades for someone who needs one. Flat is a position, and the cost of a trade you did not take is zero.

Readiness Score
A 0 to 2 score written at the top of the pre-market sheet. 2: curious about what the market will do at your levels. 1: neutral. 0: needing something from the market today. It is recorded as data for the journal, not used as a gate.

A completed sheet clears you to trade, whatever the score. Module 16 puts the score on the sheet, with one line saying why. Its job here is narrow: it lets your own record answer a question you cannot answer by introspection, which is whether the mornings you score 0 are the mornings your Broke Rule and Impulsive tags cluster. Some traders find they do; some find they don't. Only your journal knows which you are.

The rule: score yourself honestly every morning, and use the score as data in the review, never as a permission or a ban in the session.

Getting it wrong comes in two forms. Scoring a 2 every day because a 0 feels like failing. Or acting on the score: skipping a session on a 0, or trading bigger on a 2. What it reveals: the score has become a verdict on you rather than a measurement of you, and a measurement that is managed stops measuring anything. The fix: the score and its one line of reason are written as the last step of the sheet every day (Module 16), and the score never touches size: size comes from your fixed base R, the stop and the grade table (Module 14), and nothing else. In the weekly review, put the count of negative tags beside each score and read the column once a month.

Rule-Breaking Is The Only Failure

On this site, a trade lost is not a failure. A rule broken is, and it is a failure whether the trade made money or not.

The winning rule-break is the dangerous one. A trade taken from the middle of the range that happens to work pays you for doing the thing you are trying to stop doing, and the pay is the strongest training signal there is. A losing rule-break at least sends the right message. A winning one sends the wrong one, loudly, and you will remember it the next time the same impulse arrives. That is why a winning C is a warning, not a result, and why a Broke Rule tag stays on a winner.

It also means the failure is entirely in your control. You cannot decide whether the next trade wins. You can decide, every time, whether it was taken by the plan. The one who re-takes a stopped trade because they "know" the direction has not made a market error; they have made the only error this course counts.

The rule: a rule broken is tagged, whatever the result, and the count of broken rules is the first number of every weekly review.

Getting it wrong looks like leaving the Broke Rule tag off a winner, or netting a rule-break's profit against its cause. What it reveals: the result is still being allowed to grade the behaviour, so the behaviour is being rewarded. The fix: a rule-break ledger in the weekly review. List every trade tagged Broke Rule, Too Early or Impulsive, winners first, with its R beside it and the rule it broke. Total the R. The week's first number is the count; its second is that total. Neither is ever netted against the plan trades.

Losing trades are the cost of a plan; broken rules are the only failure. Every mistake your mind makes in a session has a mechanism that catches it, and that mechanism is set, written or rehearsed before the session starts.

Worked Case: The Morning After The First Win

Illustration · not a trade record

A composite MNQ morning, built to show the fixes working in order. It belongs to no one, and the prices are invented for the arithmetic. This plan's base R is $100, so the kill switch is typed into the account as $200, with the lock on. Every stop sits 2 points behind the zone's far edge (Module 14), and each trade is sized so its stop is 1R: a 12.50-point stop is 50 ticks, $25 a contract, so 4 contracts. A scheduled break sits on the plan at 11:00 to 11:15.

09:22 ET. The sheet carries two branches. Branch 1, long: if price prints two 1-minute closes below the pd-low zone (21,600.00 to 21,606.00), then a 1-minute close back above 21,606.00, and a Pause N Go fires from the reclaimed zone, then target the on-high zone, 21,660.00. Invalidation: a 5-minute close below 21,600.00. Branch 2, short, the mirror: if price prints two 1-minute closes above the on-high zone (21,660.00 to 21,666.00), then a 1-minute close back below 21,660.00, and a Pause N Go fires from the reclaimed zone, then target the pd-low zone, 21,606.00. Invalidation: a 5-minute close above 21,666.00. Anything else is the nothing case. The last step of the sheet: readiness 1, neutral, with one line why. Nothing about the plan changes because of it. The sheet is complete, so the trader is clear to trade.

09:42 ET. Branch 1. Read: failed breakdown (Module 11): two 1-minute closes below the far edge, 21,597.50 at 09:36 and 21,596.25 at 09:37, then the 09:38 bar closes back above the near edge at 21,607.00. No 5-minute close prints below 21,600.00. Trigger: the pullback touches the zone at 09:39; the 09:40 and 09:41 bars hold inside it (pause high 21,605.50) with no close below 21,600.00, and the 09:42 bar closes at 21,610.50, above the pause's high. Grade line, written before the order: pd-low, Pause N Go, stop behind the far edge, 4 contracts from the stop; A+ or A, pending the fill. Long 4 at 21,610.50; stop 21,598.00, 12.50 points (1R). Target 21,660.00, 49.50 points away, 3.96:1. Planned scale: half off at 2R, 21,635.50. The fill is 21,610.50, the intended price: A+. The scale fills at 09:49, +25.00 points on 2 contracts (+1R); the runner's target fills at 09:54, +49.50 points on 2 contracts (+1.98R). The trade closes at +2.98R before costs; execution mark: Pass.

09:58 ET. Price drifts back to 21,633.00, in the middle of the range, 27 points from each zone, and it feels like the easiest morning of the month. The hand goes to the order ticket for a second long. The grade line comes first: location, mid-range; trigger, none; stop, nothing to put it behind. Two or fewer checks: C. The line is written, "C, not taken, 09:58", and no order goes in.

10:22 ET. Branch 2. Read: failed breakout (Module 11): two 1-minute closes above the far edge, 21,667.50 at 10:16 and 21,668.25 at 10:17, then the 10:18 bar closes back below the near edge at 21,658.50. No 5-minute close prints above 21,666.00. Trigger: the pullback touches the zone at 10:19; the 10:20 and 10:21 bars hold inside it (pause low 21,660.25) with no close above 21,666.00, and the 10:22 bar closes at 21,655.50, below the pause's low. Grade line, written before the order: on-high, Pause N Go, stop behind the far edge, 4 contracts from the stop; A+ or A, pending the fill. Short 4 at 21,655.50; stop 21,668.00, 12.50 points (1R). Target 21,606.00, 49.50 points away, 3.96:1. Planned scale: half off at 2R, 21,630.50. The fill is 21,655.50, the intended price: A+.

10:31 ET. The resting stop fills at 21,668.00: −12.50 points, −1R. The trade was built to plan, so it gets no negative tag; execution mark: Pass.

10:32 ET. The reset. Every working entry order cancelled (none was working). Feet flat, one long exhale, four rounds. Aloud: price has traded back above the on-high zone; nothing on the plan is forming here, because the plan has no long branch above the on-high; a setup would need price back at the pd-low zone, with two closes below it, a reclaim and a Pause N Go. The third answer goes into the journal in one line.

10:33 to 11:00 ET. The 5-minute bar ending 10:35 closes at 21,671.00, above 21,666.00: Branch 2's invalidation, so that branch is finished for this test. Price never comes back to the pd-low zone. No trade. The morning stands at +1.98R before costs.

11:00 ET. The alarm. The break was on the plan before the open, so it is taken on a green morning exactly as it would be on a red one. The platform is closed, not minimised.

The other version of the same morning. Without the grade line at 09:58, the mid-range long goes in. Whatever it does, the next entry is a little closer to it in time and a little further from a level, and after the first loss the one after is taken to get it back. The kill switch would end that day at 2R down; it would not have saved the morning's win. Only the line at 09:58 and the reset at 10:32 do that.

Hypothetical illustration. Hypothetical results have inherent limits: they are not actual trading and do not reflect real fills, slippage or the pressure of risk, and no representation is made that any account will achieve similar results.

Notice what decided the morning. The read was the same in both versions. The −1R on the 10:22 short was a plan trade, from a branch written at 09:22, and needed no fixing. The two moments that mattered were a written line before an order that was never placed, and a routine after a loss that sent the next trade back to the chart.

Which Of These Is Yours?

Answer from your journal, not from memory, for your last ten sessions or as many as you have. Score one point for each line that is true.

  1. Reset. Every entry after a losing trade has a written third answer timestamped before it.
  2. Rehearsal. You have rehearsed the reset with your heart rate up at least once in each of the last two weeks.
  3. The second trade. No trade in the period was taken at grade C, and every grade line was written before its order.
  4. Plan losses. No losing A+ or A trade carries a negative tag.
  5. Scheduled breaks. Every plan had its break times written, and every break was taken.
  6. Readiness. A readiness score is recorded for every session, and no session's size differed because of it.

6 of 6: the mechanisms are in place; the weekly review keeps them there. 4 or 5: the first false line is next week's one fix, and only that one. Under 4: before your next session, fix line 1, and add the others one a week. One line overrides the score: if line 1 is false, it is the fix, whatever the total, because every other line on this page depends on the next trade coming from the chart.

Before Your Next Session

Apply This Now

Run the Post-Loss Reset once with no position on, straight after a flight of stairs or a fast two-minute walk: cancel orders on an empty ticket, feet flat, one long exhale, four rounds of box breathing, the three questions aloud, and the third answer written in one line in your journal with the time. That's the whole assignment.

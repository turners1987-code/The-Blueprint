MODULE 15 The Blueprint

Writing The Plan

A plan is not a guess about where price will go; it is a short list of sentences that a price can prove wrong, and any sentence no price can prove wrong doesn't belong in it.

Copies this lesson as Markdown to paste into any AI assistant

The Plan That Was A Forecast

You know the morning. You wrote a plan. It said the overnight looked firm and today should go higher. At 09:41 price dropped forty points, and you bought it, because the plan said higher. At 09:52 you bought it again. By ten o'clock you had been stopped out twice and couldn't say where the plan had told you that you were wrong, because it never said.

That wasn't a plan. It was a forecast with a direction in it, and a forecast has no price at which it ends. This page teaches you to write the plan as branches: if price does a named thing at a named level, then you do a named thing, until a named price says you were wrong. Each branch carries a conviction. One line says where you do nothing. The whole thing fits in one paragraph.

There are seven parts, mechanical first: the shape of a branch, the level and the trigger, the invalidation, the nothing case, the specificity test and the final read. The last part, conviction, is about you rather than the chart, which is why it comes last.

The Branch: If, Then, Unless

Every trade in this course traces one chain: idea → setup (location + read) → trigger → construction (level → stop → risk → size → entry). The plan is where the idea gets written down, before the session, in a form the rest of the chain can use.

Plan
What you write before a session: one or more branches and a nothing case. It says what you will do at each place price can go. It never says where price will go.
Branch
One conditional line of the plan: a level, the behaviour and trigger that switch it on, the target, the invalidation that ends it, and a conviction. On the Module 16 sheet each branch is written as one Idea line.
Idea
A conditional statement written before the session: a thesis in if/then form at a named level, the invalidation that ends it, and a conviction grade of strong, moderate or thin. It describes what you will do if price reaches a place, not where price will go.

A branch has three parts, always in this order:

  • Thesis. "If [behaviour] at [zone], and [trigger] fires, then [target level]." Four slots, each filled with something you can point to on a chart.
  • Invalidation. The price, judged on a candle close, at which the thesis is wrong.
  • Conviction. Strong, moderate or thin.

Levels are written as zones, not single lines, so a branch always knows where its far edge is.

Zone
A level written as a price band with two edges rather than a single line. Price can trade into a zone without breaking it; acceptance through the whole zone is what changes the read.
Far Edge
The edge of a zone on the far side from where price arrives. For a level above current price it is the top of the zone; for a level below, the bottom. The stop goes behind it.

A full branch reads like this: "If price accepts above the pd-high zone, 21,684 to 21,690, and a Pause N Go fires on the pullback, then prior-week-high is the target. Invalidation: a 5-minute close back below 21,684. Conviction: moderate." Nothing in it says the market will go up. It says what you do if it does a particular thing in a particular place.

The rule: every directional line of the plan is a branch: thesis, invalidation, conviction, in that order.

Getting it wrong looks like a plan that opens "NQ higher today" or "looking for longs". What it reveals: you are planning the market's actions instead of your own, so the session will be spent checking whether the market agrees with you. The fix: every directional line on the plan begins with the word "If". A line that begins with anything else is struck through before 09:25.

Every Branch Needs A Level And A Trigger

The "if" clause holds two things, and a branch needs both. The level says where. The trigger says when.

The level is a zone built from the level list in Module 06: the pre-marked ones on your sheet (pd-high, pd-val, on-low, prior-week-high and the rest), or an in-session one such as the opening range high or low once its window has closed. The behaviour at the level is a read you can see on a closed bar, such as acceptance: a 5-minute close beyond the zone within 15 minutes of the first touch (Module 10).

Trigger
The price event at the level that tells you to enter now, such as Pause N Go, a resting limit after displacement, or a level reclaim on a 5-minute close (Module 13). Order flow can confirm a trigger; it is never the trigger.

Plenty of things look like an "if" and aren't one:

  • An indicator. VWAP, pivots, moving averages, Fibonacci levels, round numbers and fair value gaps are not locations in this framework. "If price holds VWAP" names no level.
  • A day type. "If it's a trend day" is a label for the whole session. No price can confirm it at 09:45.
  • A breakout of the opening range. "If the opening range breaks, go with it" has no trigger. The OR high and low can be levels once the window closes, and then they need a trigger like any other.
  • The overnight's direction. The overnight session builds levels for your list. It never supplies the direction of a branch.
  • Order flow on its own. Absorption or failing delta can confirm a trigger at a level (Module 07). It doesn't replace the price event.

A read that Module 11 teaches as context, a rejection at a range extreme, can end a branch or cancel one. In this plan it carries no entry of its own, so it belongs in the nothing case.

The rule: every branch names a zone from the level list and a trigger from Module 13 that must fire there. A branch missing either is not written.

Getting it wrong looks like a branch with a trigger and no level: "buy the first Pause N Go after the open". What it reveals: you are planning to react to movement rather than to a place, and a trade from the middle of the range has nothing behind its stop. It fails the first check of the grade rubric in Module 14: the location is a named level. The fix: write the slug before any other word of the thesis. If you can't name a slug, the branch doesn't exist and that part of the chart goes into the nothing case.

The Invalidation Is A Price

Invalidation
The named price, judged on a candle close of a stated timeframe, at which a branch's thesis is wrong. It sits at or behind the zone's far edge. Written before the session, never moved during it.

Show it: "Invalidation: a 5-minute close back below 21,684." That line has a number, a timeframe and a direction. Tomorrow, anyone with the chart can say whether it happened, and on which bar.

The invalidation is not the stop. The stop is a resting order behind the far edge, placed at entry and sized in Module 14. The invalidation is the close that tells you the thesis is over, whether or not the stop has filled. "Thesis broken on a candle close" is one of the four reasons to flatten by hand in Module 02, and the invalidation line is where that close is defined, in advance, so the exit is the plan and not a feeling.

No Setup
The journal tag for a trade with no named location and read behind it: a trade taken on feel. An idea without a written invalidation is No Setup, however good it sounds.

The rule: every branch carries one invalidation price with its candle timeframe. No written invalidation means No Setup.

Getting it wrong looks like "out if it starts looking weak", or "below the low", with no low named. What it reveals: you haven't decided where you are wrong, so when the market says no you will argue with it, and the stop becomes the only exit you respect. The fix: an invalidation line is accepted only if it contains a number and a timeframe, such as "5-minute close". Missing either, the line is crossed out, and any trade off that branch is tagged No Setup.

The Nothing Case

Nothing Case
The line of the plan that names where no branch applies, written as a price range with two numbers, plus anything the plan has no branch for. In the nothing case the action is no trade.

Show it: "Between 21,562 and 21,684, without acceptance outside it: no trade. A gap open: no trade." It looks like the least important line on the plan. On most sessions it is the line you spend the longest inside.

The nothing case covers more than the middle of the range. Any level with no branch attached is in it. A rejection read with no branch is in it. A gap open, when no gap branch was written, is in it. Anything that is not on the plan is no trade, and writing that down turns a quiet hour from something you endure into something you are carrying out.

The rule: every plan has a nothing case written as a price range with two numbers, and it ends with the words "no trade".

Getting it wrong looks like a plan with two branches and no nothing case, then a trade at 10:15 from a level that had no branch. What it reveals: the plan covers what you hope price will do, not everything price can do, so the unplanned space gets filled by impulse. The fix: the nothing case is the one line you can't leave blank, and you say it aloud in the rehearsal on the Module 16 sheet. If you trade from inside it, the journal tag is No Setup.

The Specificity Test

Specificity Test
The check a plan line has to pass: after the session, with only the line and the chart, you can mark the bar where it triggered and the price where it ended without guessing. A line that fails is not a plan line.

Six slots, checked one at a time: a slug written as a zone; a behaviour visible on a closed bar; a trigger; a target that is a level; an invalidation price with its timeframe; a conviction. Here is what the test does to four common lines.

Each slot answers a question you will otherwise answer live, under pressure. A gap-day branch follows the same test: the gap is defined only as an RTH open outside the prior day's range, and its target slot names the PD range edge (Module 12), never the prior close.

The test also checks the geometry. Module 14 requires the trade to clear 2:1 to a target that is a level, with the stop behind the far edge. If the target slot names a level too close for that, the branch is written with its condition attached: "no trade unless the stop leaves 2:1". The arithmetic itself is done at the trigger.

The rule: a branch passes only with all six slots filled. Five of six is not a branch.

Getting it wrong looks like a plan that always seems to have been right, because every line was loose enough to fit whatever happened. What it reveals: the lines were written to stay right, and a plan that can't be wrong teaches you nothing. The fix: the evening after each session, take each branch and the chart and mark two bars: the one where it triggered (or "never") and the one where it ended. Any branch where you had to guess at either bar failed the test; rewrite it before tomorrow's plan.

The Final Read: One Paragraph

Final Read
The plan as it stands at 09:25: one paragraph made of one sentence of context in location words, one sentence per branch, and one sentence for the nothing case. No day-type label, no directional call.

On the Module 16 sheet, the Idea lines and the nothing case, read together, are the final read. It is the paragraph you say aloud in the rehearsal and the only thing you look at when price reaches a level.

Three things never go in it. A day-type label ("trend day", "range day"), because it is a forecast about the whole session that no single price can prove wrong before it has cost you. A directional call ("bullish", "expecting higher"), because once it is written you will read every bar to agree with it. A target for the day's profit, because the plan's targets are levels, not amounts.

The rule: the final read is one paragraph, finished by 09:25, with no day-type label and no directional call.

Getting it wrong comes in two forms. A plan that runs to a page reveals that you couldn't decide which branches matter, so you will decide live. A plan that opens with a label ("trend day up") reveals that the forecast came first and the branches were written to fit it. The fix: a five-sentence cap; if the paragraph runs longer, cut the branch with the thinnest conviction. Then strike these words wherever they appear: trend day, range day, bullish, bearish, expecting, should.

Conviction Is Not A Direction

Conviction
A label of strong, moderate or thin attached to one branch: how much structure and context stand behind its thesis. It is recorded for the journal. It doesn't change your size and it isn't a direction.

Many pre-market sheets carry a field called "bias", filled in with "long" or "short". The sheet in this course has no such field. In its place, each branch carries its own conviction, so a plan can hold a moderate long branch above value and a thin short branch below it, each true only if price gets there.

Conviction is counted, not felt, from the three checks in Module 09, one point each. Zone: the zone holds more than one level from your list. Path: no other level from your list sits between the zone and the target. Room and clock: the target sits inside the ATR room frame, and no Tier One release falls inside the 09:30 to 11:00 window. Three points is strong, two is moderate, one or none is thin. Write the reason as one clause naming the check that failed, such as "moderate: the htf-lvn sits in the path to the target". Then leave it alone. Conviction doesn't change your size. Size comes from the grade, in one table, in Module 14. Whether your strong branches behave differently from your thin ones is something your journal can show you, once there are enough of each.

This is the hard part, because a plan is where the wish to know gets in. You want the session to have an answer before it starts, and a single direction feels like one.

The rule: each branch carries its own conviction with a one-clause reason; conviction never appears in the size line and never stands for a direction.

Getting it wrong looks like one strong branch and nothing on the other side, or "strong" on every branch every day, or a contract added because you felt sure. What it reveals: the conviction is a belief about direction dressed as a label, and a belief is what keeps you in a trade after its invalidation has printed. The fix: write each branch backwards. Invalidation first, then the level and the trigger, then the target, and conviction last, with its reason. By the time you reach the conviction slot, you have already written down how you are wrong.

A plan is a set of branches, each a level, a trigger and a price that ends it, plus a line that says where you do nothing. If no price can prove a line wrong, it isn't a plan line, however confident it sounds.

Branch 1, written backwards. Invalidation: a 5-minute close back below 21,684. Level and trigger: if price accepts above the pd-high zone (21,684 to 21,690), meaning a 5-minute close above 21,690 within 15 minutes of the first touch, and a Pause N Go fires on the first pullback into the zone (at least two 1-minute bars holding inside it with no 1-minute close below 21,684, then a 1-minute close above the pause's high). Target: prior-week-high, 21,790. Checks: zone, one level only, no; path, no level between 21,690 and 21,790, yes; room and clock, 21,790 is inside the frame's 21,894 and no Tier One falls in the window, yes. Conviction: moderate: one level in the zone.

Branch 2. Invalidation: a 5-minute close back above 21,562. Level and trigger: if price accepts below the pd-val zone (21,556 to 21,562), meaning a 5-minute close below 21,556 within 15 minutes of the first touch, and a Pause N Go fires on the first pullback into the zone. Target: pd-low, 21,526. Checks: zone, one level only, no; path, no level between 21,556 and 21,526, yes; room and clock, 21,526 is inside the frame's 21,368 and no Tier One falls in the window, yes. Conviction: moderate: one level in the zone. The target is about 30 points away, so this branch is no trade unless the stop the structure needs at the trigger leaves 2:1.

Nothing case: between 21,562 and 21,684, without acceptance outside it: no trade. That includes the on-high, the pd-vah, settlement, the pd-vpoc and on-poc zone and the on-low; none has a branch today. A first touch of either edge that isn't accepted within 15 minutes: no trade. A gap open, above 21,690 or below 21,520: no gap branch is written, so no trade.

Final read, as it is said aloud in the rehearsal:

"Inside the PD range and inside PD value, overnight inside the range, no Tier One. If price accepts above 21,690 and a Pause N Go fires on the pullback, long toward the prior-week high at 21,790, wrong on a 5-minute close below 21,684, moderate. If price accepts below 21,556 and a Pause N Go fires on the pullback, short toward the PD low at 21,526, wrong on a 5-minute close above 21,562, moderate, and only if the stop leaves 2:1. Between 21,562 and 21,684, and on a gap open, nothing."

Four sentences. No day type, no direction, no number for the day's profit. The stop, the size and the entry come from Module 14 at the trigger, from these lines. The plan ends here, at 09:25; what happens after 09:30 belongs to Module 17.

The same morning, the same levels, written as a wish. A composite; it belongs to no one.

"Strong close yesterday and a firm overnight. Feels like a trend day up. Looking to buy dips around 21,600, target new highs, out if it starts looking weak. Bias: long."

Run it through the specificity test. Level: 21,600 sits between the on-low zone and the pd-vpoc zone and belongs to neither. Behaviour: "dips" can't be seen on a closed bar. Trigger: none. Target: "new highs" is not a level. Invalidation: "looking weak" is not a price, so this is No Setup before the open. Conviction: "feels like" is a mood, and "Bias: long" is a direction, not a conviction.

Zero of six. It also carries a day-type label and has no nothing case, so every tick between 21,562 and 21,684 is a possible entry. The structure on the chart was identical to the plan above. Only the writing changed.

Notice what the first plan didn't need. It has no opinion about whether the overnight was firm, no guess at the day's range and no prediction. Every line names a price you can see from the chair, and the line with the most room in it is the one that says "no trade".

Is Your Plan A Plan?

Take your last five written plans. Score each one, a point per line that is true for every branch on it:

  1. Level: a slug from the level list, written as a zone with both edges.
  2. Behaviour: a read you can see on a closed bar, such as acceptance.
  3. Trigger: a named trigger from Module 13 that must fire in the zone.
  4. Target: a level, not "higher" or a number of points.
  5. Invalidation: a price with its candle timeframe.
  6. Conviction: strong, moderate or thin, counted from the three checks, with a one-clause reason naming the check that failed.
  7. Nothing case: a price range with two numbers, ending "no trade".
  8. Final read: one paragraph, no day-type label, no directional call.

8 of 8 is a plan. Anything less is not yet one. The threshold: if any of your five plans missed line 5, the trades taken from it were No Setup on the plan's own terms, and the invalidation line is the first thing to fix. If two or more of the five scored under 8 for any other reason, find the line you miss most often and fix that one before writing another branch.

Before Your Next Session

Apply This Now

Tonight, write one branch for tomorrow, backwards: the invalidation price first, then the zone and the trigger, then the target level, then the conviction with its one-clause reason. One sentence. That's the whole assignment.

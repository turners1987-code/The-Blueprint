MODULE 23 Capstone

Building Your Own PlayBook

Your PlayBook is not a list of the setups you like: it is a short written record of the few trades you actually take, with numbers that came from your own data, tight enough that your journal can grade you against it.

A Framework Is Not Yet A Book

By now you have the pieces: levels, reads, triggers, construction, the hard rules, the routine, the journal and a way to test an idea. And if someone asked you at 09:20 what you trade, you would still answer with a paragraph. Some of it from this course, some from a video last month, a stop size you copied from someone, a footprint setting you never chose, and two setups you took once each and still think about.

That pile can't be graded. When a session goes wrong you can't tell whether the plan failed or you left it, because the plan was never written in a form that could be left. This page turns the pile into a book: a few pages, each one a trade you actually take, each one carrying numbers you derived yourself and a line in your own register. Nothing on this page describes anyone else's book. Every field is yours to fill.

There are seven parts. The first two give the book its shape: the working set and the page. The next four fill it with your own numbers and your own evidence: base R, the stop floor, footprint settings and the register. The last part is about you, not the chart: executing a book you wrote and letting the journal, not your feeling about the week, say how you did.

Write Your Working Set

PlayBook
Your own written book of trades: one page per setup you actually take, each with the same fixed fields, plus a parking lot for everything else. It describes what you do at a level, never where price will go.
Working Set
The short list of setup, location and trigger combinations you actually take, written in the course vocabulary (TAXONOMY slugs). Only a combination on this list may become a trade. Everything else goes to the parking lot.
Parking Lot
The dated list in your PlayBook of ideas you are not trading: setups you saw elsewhere, variations, filters, new levels. An idea leaves the parking lot only at a scheduled review, and only into your register, never straight onto a page.

Every trade in this course traces one chain: idea → setup (location + read) → trigger → construction (level → stop → risk → size → entry). Your working set is that chain with the blanks filled in, one line per combination you take. Written in the course vocabulary, a working set might read like this, for example:

  • trend-continuation · pd-low · pause-n-go
  • trend-continuation · on-high · pause-n-go
  • trend-continuation · pd-vah · resting-limit

Three slots, each one a slug from TAXONOMY: a setup from section 2, a location from section 4, a trigger from section 3. A confirmation, if you use one, is recorded with the trade, not written into the line; it confirms a trigger and never replaces it (Module 07). A line that needs a word the vocabulary doesn't have is not a working-set line yet. It goes to the parking lot.

The list comes from your journal, not from your hopes. Open the last few weeks of trades and write down the combinations you actually took more than once. That is your working set as it stands today, and it is usually shorter than you expect. Some reads in this course carry no entry at all: Range Rejection is taught in Module 11 as a context read, so in your book it shows up as a reason a branch is cancelled or a target is chosen, not as a page with a trigger. If you want to write an entry for it, that entry is a new idea, and new ideas go to the parking lot first.

The rule: the working set is written in slugs, one line per combination, taken from your own journal; any trade off the list is a trade off the book.

Getting it wrong looks like a working set of eleven lines, or one that says "breakouts and reversals", or a list that includes something you saw last Tuesday and haven't taken yet. What it reveals: the list is a record of what interests you, not of what you do, so the journal has nothing fixed to grade against. The fix: a line goes on the working set only if your journal shows you took it at least twice under its slugs; every other idea is written in the parking lot with the date you added it. At each scheduled review, read the parking lot once and move at most one item into your register. Nothing moves in the middle of a week.

One Page Per Setup, Same Fields Every Time

PlayBook Page
One page of your PlayBook for one working-set line, filled in with the same nine fields every time: name, location, read, trigger, construction, invalidation, worked composite, status in your register, and last review date. A page with an empty field is a draft, not a page.

Every page has the same fields in the same order. The order is the chain: where, what price does there, what fires the entry, how the trade is built, where it is wrong, what it looks like, and what your own evidence says about it.

FieldWhat goes in itIt fails if

LocationThe TAXONOMY section 4 slug, how the zone is drawn, its session scope and which edge is the far edge.It names an indicator, a round number or "support".
ReadWhat price must do at the zone, visible on a closed bar, with its timeframe and time limit, plus the condition notes: what cancels the page.It can only be judged after the move, or it includes a day-type call.
TriggerThe price event from Module 13 that fires the entry, defined bar by bar, and the confirmation sub-form you record if you use one.Order flow alone is the trigger, or the trigger is "when it looks ready".

A page is short: it should fit on one side of a sheet. Its job is to be read in the minute before a session and in the minute before an order, and to be the standard the journal holds you to afterwards.

The rule: one page per working-set line, all nine fields filled, in this order; a draft with an empty field is not traded.

Getting it wrong looks like a page that is a screenshot with arrows on it, or a construction field that says "stop below the low" with no low named. What it reveals: the page was written to remind you of a trade you liked, not to define one, so in the moment you will fill the empty fields with whatever the chart is doing. The fix: run the specificity test from Module 15 on each field. A week after writing a page, take it and the chart of a session you have not studied: if you have to guess the bar where the read, the trigger or the invalidation happened, that field is rewritten before the page is traded again.

Your Base R Comes From Your Account, And Then It Stops Moving

Base R
The fixed amount you risk on a full-size trade, chosen once from your account and the losing run you can sit through. Every result is measured in it. It does not rise after a good week or fall after a bad one; it changes only at a scheduled review, written as a new line on the page.

Base R is the one number in the book that comes from you rather than from the market. Derive it in three written lines:

  1. The losing run. Losses arrive in runs even when every trade is independent (Module 20). Write down a run of full losses you intend to be able to take without changing anything: a number of trades in a row, chosen before you know your own record.
  2. The kill switch. Your daily max loss is 2R, set at the account level (Module 02). Write the number of full kill-switch days in a row you intend to be able to take.
  3. The amount. Pick the R at which the larger of those two totals is a loss your account survives and you would still trade the next morning exactly as written. If at that R your stops would usually need zero contracts, the account is telling you the instrument size, not asking you to raise R (Module 03).

Then size comes from base R and the stop, never the other way round: base R divided by the stop in ticks times the tick value, rounded down. A wider stop means fewer contracts, never more risk. The grade decides how much of base R a trade gets, from the one table in Module 14: A+ and A trade base R, B trades half, C is no trade. Choosing the amount itself is Module 14's job; this page only asks that it is yours, written down, and fixed.

The rule: base R is derived once from your account and the losing run you wrote down, written on the first page of the book, and used unchanged on every trade until a scheduled review changes it.

Getting it wrong looks like a base R that went up after three green days, or down after a kill-switch day, or a size picked because "this one feels good". What it reveals: risk is being set by your recent results, so your R column measures your moods as much as your trades, and two months of results can't be compared with each other. The fix: write base R and its date on the first page of the book; the journal's size line is checked against it on every trade. A change is made only at a scheduled review, written as a new dated line, never in a session, and never in the week after a large win or a kill-switch day.

Your Stop Floor Comes From Your Own Adverse Excursion

Adverse Excursion (MAE)
How far price went against a trade or an occurrence before it ended, measured from the entry price, as a positive number of ticks. The journal records it as mae_ticks on every trade.
Stop Floor
The smallest stop distance, in ticks, that one of your pages will accept, derived from your own adverse-excursion record for that page and session block. The stop goes behind the far edge first; if the far edge is closer than the floor, the stop goes out to the floor and size falls.

The stop goes where the read is wrong: behind the far edge of the zone. But a stop can sit behind structure and still sit inside the ordinary back-and-forth of the market, where it gets hit by noise before the read has had a chance to be right or wrong. A floor is the minimum distance below which your stop is inside your own noise. You don't borrow it. Nobody else's tick count describes your page, your level or your time of day.

The method. Write the floor rule before you sort anything. Then:

  1. Collect occurrences of one page: live trades, sim trades, replay and chart review all count, because you are measuring price, not fills. For each one, record the adverse excursion in ticks from the trigger price until the read either reached its target or printed its invalidation.
  2. Record the session block (early, mid or late) on every occurrence, and keep the blocks apart. If your own table differs by block, each block gets its own floor.
  3. Split the occurrences into the ones that reached their target and the ones that were invalidated. Sort the first group by adverse excursion.
  4. Apply the rule you wrote in advance to the earlier half of your occurrences, then check the number it gives against the later half.

A made-up dataset, so you can see the arithmetic. Twelve occurrences of one page in the early block. Eight reached their target, with adverse excursions of 14, 18, 22, 22, 26, 31, 35 and 44 ticks. Four were invalidated: 30, 52, 60 and 71. The rule written beforehand: "the floor is the smallest whole number of ticks that holds the adverse excursion of six of every eight occurrences that reached their target." Sorted, the sixth value is 31, so the floor is 32 ticks. A stop at 20 ticks would have been run on six of the eight that went on to work: that stop was inside the noise, not behind the read. These numbers are invented and twelve occurrences is far too few; the method is the point, and the count goes on.

Two cautions. The floor is a rule in its own right, so it gets its own entry in your register, apart from the page it protects; a page tested with one stop says nothing about the same page with another. And a floor goes stale: re-measure it on the review schedule, because the market's range changes and last quarter's floor can be run by this quarter's noise.

The rule: the stop goes behind the far edge or at your floor for that page and block, whichever is wider; size comes from that distance; if the trade no longer clears 2:1 to its target, there is no trade.

Getting it wrong looks like a stop tightened to fit more contracts, or a floor copied from a post, or a floor chosen after staring at the sorted list until a number looked right. What it reveals: the stop is being set by what you want to risk or what you hope the trade does, not by where your own record says noise ends, and a stop inside noise makes every later diagnosis of the page wrong. The fix: keep a floor table on the page: block, floor, the rule that produced it, the occurrence count and the date measured. The construction field reads the floor from that table. A floor with no rule written above it is struck through.

Your Footprint Settings Start From A Default And Become Yours

Working Default
A setting you start from because you need one, not because a test chose it. For the footprint stacked-imbalance read, the course's working default is ratio 5 and minimum delta 15, chosen for screen clarity. You replace it with your own setting, derived from your own record.

If a page uses a footprint confirmation, or the stacked-imbalance location, the settings on your footprint chart decide what you see. The imbalance ratio and the minimum delta change how many cells light up, and so how often the read appears on your screen. The working default, ratio 5 and minimum delta 15, was picked so the chart is readable, not because any test chose it. It is a place to start.

From there, the setting becomes yours the same way the floor does. Write the setting on the page and save it as a named chart template, so every trade records which template it was read on. Change one parameter at a time, only at a scheduled review, and when you change it, the occurrences under the old setting stay a separate cohort; they don't carry over to the new one. To compare two settings fairly, replay the same occurrences under each and count where the read would and would not have appeared.

One fact about testing belongs on the page, so you don't mistake it for a fact about the read. A footprint read needs tick data, and the tick history a retail platform keeps is short. Without bought tick data, you cannot test a footprint setting across several years, the way a price-only rule can be tested. Your own forward record is the evidence you can build, one occurrence at a time.

The rule: start from the working default, write the setting and the template name on the page, record the template on every trade that uses it, and change one parameter at a time at a scheduled review.

Getting it wrong looks like lowering the ratio mid-session until a stack appears where you wanted one, or three templates in use in one week with nothing on the trades to say which. What it reveals: the setting is being chosen by the trade you want to take, so the footprint is confirming your wish, not the market. The fix: one saved template per page, named with its settings, and the template name recorded with the confirmation sub-form on every trade. A trade read on an unnamed or edited template is graded as if the confirmation was absent.

Run Your Own Register

Register Entry
One numbered, dated page in your own register for one idea: the rule as locked before any data was opened, the null it is tested against, its status, its n (live and sim shown apart), and its reopen condition if it failed. A change to the rule is a new entry, never an edit.
Register Status
The label your own register gives one of your ideas, from your own tests. Untested: written, not yet run against data. Watch: positive but unstable, or too few occurrences. Candidate: beats your null and holds in both halves, but is below one of your gates. Validated: passed both of your gates. Killed: failed, with its reason and reopen condition written. It describes your record of your idea, nothing else.
Reopen Condition
The written condition under which an idea that failed in your register may be tested again, recorded with the reason it failed. Reopening creates a new register entry with a new rule; the old entry and its result stay as they are.

Module 22 taught how to test an idea: lock the rule, choose the null, split the sample, set the gates. The register is where you keep the results of doing that to your own ideas, one entry per idea. Every page in your book points to an entry. The entry, not the page, carries the evidence, and the page copies only one thing from it: the label in its status field, with the entry number.

An entry has six lines, written in this order and never rewritten:

  1. Number and date. Entries are numbered in the order you write them.
  2. The rule, locked. Exact enough that someone else could run it, written before you open the data.
  3. The null. What the same rule would produce from randomness that keeps the market's own character (Module 22).
  4. Status. The label your gates give it today, in the register's own vocabulary.
  5. n. Occurrences and trades, live and sim counted apart and never pooled, because a simulator fills a resting limit on a touch.
  6. Reopen condition. Written the day an idea fails: why it failed and what would justify a new test.

Three habits keep a register honest. Anything that changes a rule, whether a filter, a skip condition, a different stop or a new time limit, is a new entry with a new number, because a rule refined after looking at the results has been fitted, not tested. An idea that failed is never retested blind: its reopen condition says what would have to be different, and a reopening is a new entry. And a parameter search, if you run one, runs on an earlier window only, with the later window kept back to check the choice. A backtest result is an upper bound on what you will get live.

The rule: every page in the book points to one register entry; the register is append-only, and any change to a rule is a new numbered entry.

Getting it wrong looks like a register entry edited after the test came back flat, a stop "adjusted" inside an old entry until the numbers improved, or an idea retested every few weeks hoping for a different answer. What it reveals: the register is being used to rescue ideas rather than to judge them, which means the label on every page is a story you told yourself. The fix: keep the register in a file where you only ever add lines at the bottom; each entry's first line is its number and date. A page whose entry number doesn't exist, or whose rule differs from its entry's rule, is a draft until a new entry is written for it.

Execute It Yourself, And Let The Journal Grade You

The last part is the hardest, because by now the book is written and the only thing left in it is you. A page you wrote from your own record is still just a page until you take it, live, at the size it says, exactly as written, enough times for the journal to say something about it.

The journal from Module 18 does the grading. Every trade carries its setup, location and trigger slugs, so every trade can be matched to a page or seen not to match one. The grade goes on at entry, from the four checks and before the outcome; the execution mark goes on after the exit. A trade whose combination is not in your working set shows up in a cohort of its own in the weekly review: that cohort is the cost of trading outside the book, counted in R. Live and sim stay apart. Sim counts occurrences of a page; only live trades, with their real fills, measure your execution of it. The gap between your intended price and your actual fill is recorded on every trade, because that is where a page you understand and a page you can execute come apart.

Then comes the part that has nothing to do with the chart. A book you copied fails at the order ticket. You hesitate on a page you never vetted yourself, because the conviction behind it was borrowed, and borrowed conviction doesn't survive the first run of losses. That run arrives whether the page is good or not, and when it does you start looking at other people's books. Each switch resets the count, so nothing ever gets a fair trial, and the journal never reaches a sample big enough to tell you anything. The trader with the most pages and the least record is the one this section is written for.

The only defence is a separation you set up in advance. In the session you are the trader: you execute the pages as written, and any idea for changing them goes in the parking lot with the time. At the scheduled review you are the one who changes the book: you read the journal by page, at the display floor, and you change at most one thing. A cohort below 30 trades and 20 distinct sessions is shown dimmed and read as a story, not a verdict.

The rule: trade only pages in your book, at base R and the grade table, exactly as written; change the book only at a scheduled review, from the journal, one change at a time.

Getting it wrong looks like a new setup adopted in the middle of a week because someone posted a winner with it, a page dropped after three losses, or a page you never take because it "doesn't feel right live". What it reveals: confidence is being sought from outside your own record, so the book changes with your mood and the record never accumulates. The fix: the trader-and-reviewer split, written on the first page of the book: in the session, ideas go to the parking lot with a timestamp and nothing else; the review is on a fixed day in your calendar, never called by a result; each review ends with one written change or the line "no change". A page you haven't taken in a month gets one question at the review: is it in the working set because you take it, or because you wish you did?

Your PlayBook is a handful of pages written in the course vocabulary, with numbers you derived from your own data and a register entry behind every page. No one else's book, record or label can tell you what a page is worth; only your own journal of executing it can.

Notice what the page doesn't contain. No result, no win rate, no claim about how often price accepts above an overnight high. The status field holds your register's label for an idea you have not counted yet, and it will change only when your own register changes it. What the page does contain is enough to grade a trade against: if a trade off this page went in without acceptance, or before the resumption close, or with a stop inside 21,742, the journal can say so.

Does Your Book Exist?

Answer from what is written down, not from what you know. Two parts: the book, then each page.

The book. Score one point for each line that is true.

  1. Your working set is written, in slugs, one line per combination.
  2. A dated parking lot exists, and nothing has moved out of it outside a scheduled review.
  3. Base R is written with its date on the first page, and every trade in your last ten sessions used it with the grade table.
  4. Every working-set line has a page, and every page points to a register entry that exists.
  5. Your review is on a fixed day in your calendar, and the last one ended with one written change or "no change".

Each page. For each page, count its nine fields: name, location, read, trigger, construction, invalidation, worked composite, status in your register, last review date. A field counts only if it passes the "it fails if" column in the template above.

The threshold. The book: 5 of 5 means it exists and the review keeps it that way; 3 or 4 means the first false line is this week's one fix; under 3 means you are trading from a pile, and line 1 is written before your next session. Each page: 9 of 9 is a page. A page missing its location, its trigger or its invalidation is not a page at all; that line comes off the working set until those fields are written, and a trade taken from a page with no written invalidation is No Setup. A page missing any other field stays on the book and gets one field fixed per review.

Before Your Next Session

Apply This Now

Open your journal and write the setup, location and trigger slugs of your last ten trades, one line each. Any combination that appears at least twice is a working-set line; write every other one in a parking lot with today's date. That's the whole assignment.

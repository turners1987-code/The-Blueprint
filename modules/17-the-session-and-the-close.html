MODULE 17 Process

The Session And The Close

Your trading day is decided before 09:30 and judged after 16:00; the hours in between are the part of it that needs you least.

Six Hours Of Watching, Nothing To Show

You know the day. The plan was done by 09:25, the levels were marked, and then you watched every 1-minute candle for six and a half hours. By 13:40 you took a trade from the middle of the range because something had to happen. At 16:05 the platform was still open, the day's trades were a blur, and the journal said only what the P&L said.

Nothing on that day was a market problem. The session had no shape you had chosen, and the day had no end. This page gives it both: how you spend the hours between the plan and the bell, how you stop at the bell, and the routine that turns the day into a record within an hour of the close. Six parts, mechanical first: alerts at levels, the plan as the only input, the close, the end-of-day routine, the screenshot and recording review, and the mental close.

Alerts At Levels, Not Eyes On Every Candle

Your plan marks levels as zones, and the stop on any trade from a zone goes behind its far edge. Nothing on your plan happens anywhere else. So the chart between your zones doesn't need you, and the platform can watch it for you.

Level Alert
A sound-on alert set on a marked zone before the session. Each zone carries two: one at the edge price will reach first, which calls you to the screen, and one at the far edge, which tells you price has traded through the whole zone.

Show it. Price is 60 points above a PD Low zone. You are not watching it fall. When it reaches the zone's top edge, the first alert sounds and you come to the screen: now the level test is happening, and the trigger can only fire from here. If the far-edge alert sounds, price has traded through the zone. Your read on that level has to account for it, and if you are in a trade from it, your stop is the next thing price meets.

In NinjaTrader 8, alerts can be built from a drawing object on the chart: click the line or rectangle you drew at the level, choose Alert, and set the action to Play Sound. The rearm setting decides whether an alert can fire again after the first time; choose it on purpose, so a second test of the same zone is not missed.

Source: NinjaTrader, NT8 Help Guide, Alerts and Configuring Alerts, read 2026-10-31. Menus change; NinjaTrader's current page wins.

The rule: every zone on the plan has its two alerts set before 09:25, sound on. Between alerts, the chart is not your job.

Getting it wrong looks like eyes on every candle from the open to the close, or a missed test because the alert was never set. What it reveals: you don't trust your levels to be where the trade is, so you look for trades everywhere. The fix: the last line of your pre-market routine is "alerts set: yes/no", with the number of zones beside it. A zone without both alerts is not on the plan.

During The Session, The Plan Is The Only Input

The session adds nothing to the plan. It only tells you which part of it is now in play. A trade is a marked level reached, a trigger fired, and a construction that fits: level, stop, risk, size, entry. Nothing else asks for an order: not a big candle, not a chat room, not a level you noticed at 12:15.

Show it. At 12:15 price bounces hard off a spot that is on no plan. It looks like a level. It isn't one of yours, so it gets a line in the day's notes ("12:15, bounce at an unmarked price") for the mark-up, and no order. In-session levels from Module 06's list, such as the opening range high and low once that window has closed, count only if the plan named them as levels to watch before the session.

The same goes for an open trade. It ends at its target, its planned scale, its stop, or by your hand for one of the four reasons in Module 02: stop hit, a Tier One release within five minutes, thesis broken on a candle close, session over. A trade that is "taking too long" has none of those. Time in a trade is not a reason to leave it, and an exit for it is Too Early.

The rule: no entry from a level that is not on the plan, and no manual exit outside the four reasons.

Getting it wrong looks like a midday entry from a price you found on the screen, or a stalled trade closed because you were bored of it. What it reveals: the screen became the plan the moment the plan went quiet. The fix: keep a notes line open beside the chart and write anything the session shows you there, with the time. Writing it is the action you take. Every entry's location must match a level on the plan's list, and every manual exit gets one of the four reasons or the Too Early tag.

The Close: Nothing New At 15:55, Platform Shut At 16:00

The day in this framework ends at 16:00 ET, a convention chosen in Module 02, not a market fact. The futures keep trading after it. You don't. The last five minutes exist so that 16:00 arrives with nothing left to decide.

Show it. At 15:55 an alarm sounds. You cancel any working entry order and take nothing new, even if an alert fires at a marked zone at 15:57. If a trade is still open, its bracket keeps working until 16:00; at 16:00 you flatten it by hand, with the reason "session over", and its execution mark is Pass. Then you close the platform. Not minimise it: close it.

If your account's firm or broker requires you flat earlier than 16:00, its time replaces 16:00 on your plan, and your 15:55 alarm moves back by the same amount. Module 03 covers allowed hours on funded accounts.

The rule: nothing new from 15:55; flat and the platform closed at 16:00 ET.

Getting it wrong looks like an entry at 15:58 "because the level was right there", or a platform left open after the close with one more chart to look at. What it reveals: the day had no end you had decided on, so the screen decided it. The fix: two alarms, set once and repeating every trading day: 15:55 labelled "nothing new" and 16:00 labelled "flat, platform shut". An entry time after 15:55 is tagged Broke Rule in the journal.

The End-Of-Day Routine

End-Of-Day Routine
The fixed routine run within an hour of the 16:00 ET close, on every trading day whatever the result: the chart mark-up, the post-exit fields and execution mark for every trade, and one line on the day.

The routine runs on a schedule, not on a result. A red day doesn't call for a longer one, and a green day doesn't skip it. It takes ten to twenty minutes on a day with a few trades, and it is done while the session is still clear in your memory.

1. The chart mark-up.

Chart Mark-Up
A written pass over the day's chart, level by level: what price did at each marked level, what you did there, and, for every trade you exited, whether price reached the planned target after your exit.

Go through the plan's levels one at a time. For each, one line: reached or not, the time, what price did there (held, traded through, never came), and what you did (trade, no trigger, no trade because it was after 15:55). Then, for each trade, look at where price went after the exit and write whether it reached the target you planned. That line is a research note for your own record. It never changes the execution mark: a Pass exit that left a move behind is still Pass, and a Fail exit that saved you a loss is still Fail. After the exit is not your money.

2. The post-exit fields and the execution mark. For every trade, complete the post-exit half of the journal as Module 18 lays it out: the exit, the R multiple, the time in trade, how far it went against you and for you, and the execution mark. Pass means the exit was one the plan allowed: target, planned scale, thesis invalidation set at entry, stop, or session end. Fail means anything else. The grade was set at entry and is not revisited now.

3. One line on the day. One sentence of fact about how the day went against the plan: "Two trades, both at plan levels; one Fail, a manual exit on a stall." Not how the market behaved, and not how you feel about the P&L.

The rule: the routine is finished within an hour of the close, every trading day, with every trade's post-exit fields and mark filled in.

Getting it wrong looks like a routine that runs only after losing days, or trades whose mark is filled in on Sunday from memory. What it reveals: the review has become a reaction to the result, so it measures how the day felt rather than what you did. The fix: a repeating alarm at a fixed time inside the hour after the close, labelled "close routine", and a checklist with three boxes in the order above. A trade with an empty mark the next morning is the first thing you fill in, before the pre-market routine starts.

Screenshots And The Recording

The mark-up shows you where price went. It cannot show you what you saw at the moment you acted, or how long you took. The screenshot and the recording can.

The screenshot. One per trade, entry and exit marked on the chart, saved with the trade. On it, in a line or two: the level, the trigger, why the stop and target sat where they did, and the exit reason. Under a minute each. Over months it becomes the folder that shows what one of your A trades looks like, which is the reference you use when a quiet market makes a weaker trade look acceptable.

The recording. Record your screen for the whole session with any screen recorder, then watch only the minutes around each trade, and around any alert you came to the screen for and did not trade. Watch for what a chart can't give you back: when the information arrived, how long you took to act, and whether what you did matches the reason you wrote down. The minutes around a stop-out and around a manual exit come first.

The rule: one annotated screenshot per trade, every day; the recording is watched for at least the minutes around each trade, as part of the routine.

Getting it wrong looks like a recording saved every day and never opened, or screenshots of winners only. What it reveals: the review is selecting what is comfortable to see. The fix: name each clip with the date and the trade's entry time, so the journal entry and the clip match one to one, and add a checkbox to the routine: "clip watched: yes/no" for every trade. No checkbox ticked, routine not finished.

The Mental Close

Mental Close
The single written sentence that ends the trading day: trades taken, how many marked Pass, and that tomorrow starts from its own plan. It is the last step of the end-of-day routine.

The platform being shut doesn't mean the day is over. The day goes on running in your head: the exit you would take back, the move you missed at 14:10, the number on the account. Carried into the evening, it becomes tomorrow's first trade, taken to make up for today. The mental close is the step that ends it, and it is written so that it doesn't depend on your mood.

Show it. "Day closed: two trades, one Pass, one Fail logged. Tomorrow starts from tomorrow's plan." That's the whole sentence. It has a count, a mark and an end. It doesn't promise to make anything back, and it doesn't grade the day by its result.

The rule: the routine ends with one sentence in the journal that starts "Day closed:", and after it, no charts until the pre-market routine.

Getting it wrong looks like reopening the chart at 20:00 to see where price went, or a sentence that ends "get it back tomorrow". What it reveals: the day is still the unit, rather than the trade, and tomorrow is already being traded against today. The fix: the sentence follows a fixed template (count, marks, "tomorrow starts from tomorrow's plan"), and anything outside it is crossed out. If the pull to look again is strong, write it as one line in the notes ("wanted to reopen the chart at 20:00") and leave it there; the weekly review will show you how often it happens.

The plan decides the session, the clock ends it, and the routine turns it into a record. In between, your job is to answer the alerts, not to watch the screen.

Worked Case: One Session, Closed

Illustration · not a trade record

A composite MNQ session, built to show the routine. It belongs to no one. Base risk is 1R; the plan marks four zones: PD Low, PD High, ON High and PD VAH, each with its two alerts set by 09:20.

09:46 ET. The near-edge alert at the PD Low zone sounds. The trader comes to the screen, the trigger fires, and they go long with the stop behind the zone's far edge. At 09:53 the far-edge alert sounds, then the resting stop fills: −1R. They run the post-loss reset from Module 02.

10:31 ET. The PD High alert sounds. A trigger fires and they go short, stop behind the far edge, target at the PD VAH, 2.4R away. By 11:05 the trade sits at +0.8R and has gone sideways for twenty minutes. They close it by hand. None of the four reasons applies, so it is tagged Too Early.

12:15 ET. A sharp bounce at a price that is on no plan. One line in the notes, no order.

15:55 ET. The "nothing new" alarm. No orders are working. At 15:57 the PD VAH alert sounds; it is logged, nothing is taken. At 16:00 they are flat, and the platform is closed.

16:20 ET, the routine.

  • Mark-up. PD Low: reached 09:46, traded through the zone by 09:53; long, stopped. PD High: reached 10:31, held; short, closed by hand at 11:05. Price reached the planned target at PD VAH at 15:57, nearly five hours after the exit. ON High: never reached. PD VAH: reached 15:57, no trade, after 15:55.
  • Post-exit fields and mark. Trade 1: exit at the stop, −1R, seven minutes in the trade, mark Pass. Trade 2: manual exit, +0.8R, 34 minutes in the trade, tagged Too Early, mark Fail. The target being reached later changes neither mark.
  • One line on the day. "Two trades, both at plan levels; one Fail, a manual exit on a stall."
  • Screenshots and recording. Two screenshots saved with the trades. The clip from 10:58 to 11:06 shows the hand moving to the exit button after a stretch of looking at the account, not the chart.
  • Mental close, 16:41. "Day closed: two trades, one Pass, one Fail logged. Tomorrow starts from tomorrow's plan."

Hypothetical illustration. Hypothetical results have inherent limits: they are not actual trading and do not reflect real fills, slippage or the pressure of risk, and no representation is made that any account will achieve similar results.

Notice what the routine did and didn't do. The −1R was a planned trade that lost, and it got no negative tag. The +0.8R was a Fail, and the mark-up made that visible without anyone having to feel bad about it. Where price went after the exit was written down as a fact for your own record. It is not your money, and it changed nothing in the marks.

How Does Your Day End?

Answer for your last five trading sessions. Score one point for each line that was true on all five.

  1. Alerts. Every zone on the plan had its two alerts set before 09:25.
  2. Plan only. Every entry was from a level on that day's plan.
  3. The close. No entry after 15:55, and the platform was closed at 16:00 ET.
  4. On schedule. The end-of-day routine was finished within an hour of the close, on green and red days alike.
  5. Every trade marked. Every trade had its post-exit fields, its execution mark and a screenshot by the end of the routine.
  6. Day closed. Each day ended with the one written sentence, and no chart was reopened before the next pre-market routine.

The threshold: 6 of 6, keep going. 4 or 5: the missing lines are the first thing you set up before your next session. Under 4: the session is still running you; start with line 3 and line 5, because one ends the day and the other makes it a record. A miss on line 3 counts as under 4 whatever the total.

Before Your Next Session

Apply This Now

After tomorrow's close, write one sentence in your journal that starts "Day closed:" and gives the number of trades, how many were marked Pass, and "tomorrow starts from tomorrow's plan". That's the whole assignment.

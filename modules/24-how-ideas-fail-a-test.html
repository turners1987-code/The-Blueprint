MODULE 24 Capstone

How Ideas Fail A Test

A popular trading idea rarely fails because it is foolish: it fails at one particular step of a test, and knowing in advance which step is built to catch it tells you more than anyone's report of how it did.

The Idea That Arrives With A Number

You will meet these ideas whether you look for them or not. The opening-range breakout. The gap that "has to fill" back to yesterday's close. The open that tells you what kind of day it is. The line price "gets to most days". The fade at ten o'clock. And the rule that looked ordinary until someone added one filter. Each one arrives with a confident sentence, often a percentage, and a chart where it went perfectly.

You can't use their number. It was counted on someone else's definition, window and stop, and you can't see how many versions were tried before that one was shown. This page doesn't replace their number with another: it states no result for any of these ideas, from this site or from anywhere. What it gives you is the step of the Module 22 test each idea is most exposed to, so that when you write one into your own register, you know where to look first.

Every trade in this course traces one chain: idea → setup (location + read) → trigger → construction (level → stop → risk → size → entry). A popular idea usually arrives as a fragment of that chain: a trigger with no read, an entry with no location, a target doing an entry's job, a label in place of an idea. The fragment is where it breaks. There are six parts, mechanical first: the null, the noise, the definition, the magnet, the lock, and last the hardest, the filter you add because you like the idea.

Failure Mode
The specific step of a test at which an idea breaks: no null, a stop inside the noise, a condition that is a different event or cannot be checked at entry, a target taken for an entry, halves or years that disagree, or a filter fitted to the data. It is written as the reason in a failed register entry.
Reopen Condition
The specific new evidence or new rule that would justify testing a failed idea again, written into its register entry at the moment it fails. A reopened idea is a new entry, tested from scratch.

Opening-Range Breakouts: No Null

Opening Range (OR)
The high and low of the session from 09:30 ET to the end of a window you choose and write on your plan. They become levels, or-high and or-low, only once the window has closed.
ORB (Opening Range Breakout)
A retired term. The opening range's high and low can be levels once the window closes; a breakout of the range is not a setup in this course. Module 24 covers how such ideas are tested.

Show it on a composite MNQ morning with invented prices. The window is 09:30 to 09:45 ET, the one this course's examples use; you set yours once and keep it. The range closes at 21,610 to 21,655. At 10:05 a 1-minute bar closes at 21,659, above the OR high, and price runs to 21,690. The idea says: buy the break, and look how often it follows through.

"How often it follows through" is a count with nothing beside it. A range drawn on the first fifteen minutes is small next to the rest of the session, so price leaves it, on one side or both, on many sessions for no reason except that the market moves. Some of those breaks run. A breakout rule scored against zero, or against 50%, is credited with all of that. The Module 22 null is built for exactly this: the same rule, same window, same entry, stop and target, run on sessions rebuilt from their own 1-minute returns shuffled within 30-minute blocks. The shuffle keeps the opening half hour wide, so shuffled sessions break their opening range too, and some of those breaks run as well. What the rule earns above that is the only part that belongs to it.

Null (Block-Shuffled Null)
The same locked rule run on price paths rebuilt from each session's own 1-minute returns, shuffled within 30-minute blocks. Shuffling destroys any real sequence while keeping the session's volatility. Its average expectancy across many shuffles is the baseline the rule must beat.

The rule: a breakout idea is written as a locked paragraph with the OR window in it, run beside its block-shuffled null, and counts only by what it earns above the null. In your plan, the OR high and low are locations once the window closes, places where a setup can happen; the break itself is a price event, not a setup.

Getting it wrong looks like "it followed through on most of the mornings you checked", or a follow-through percentage compared with a coin flip. What it reveals: you are counting the market's ordinary movement as the rule's work, which is the one thing a breakout rule can't take credit for. The fix: the null goes in the locked paragraph and the report has a null column that can't be left blank. The OR window is written in the same paragraph; a run with a different window, chosen after looking at the first, is a new entry with a new number.

The Fixed-Time Fade: A Stop Inside The Noise

Fixed-Time Fade
A popular idea that enters against the morning's move at a set clock time, such as 10:00 ET, with no level at the entry. Written as a rule it can be run through a test; it is not a setup in this course, because a clock time is not a location.

Show it with invented numbers. The idea: at 10:00 ET, if price is above the 09:30 open, sell, with a stop a fixed 20 ticks (5 points) above the entry and the open as the target. At 10:00 price is 21,682. The stop goes at 21,687. Nothing happened at 21,682 before the order; the clock chose it. So nothing sits behind the stop either, and it fails the stop-structure test in Module 06.

Adverse Excursion (MAE)
How far price went against a trade or an occurrence before it ended, measured from the entry price, as a positive number of ticks. The journal records it as mae_ticks on every trade.

Now put that stop next to your own record. In Module 23 you derive a stop floor from your own adverse excursion: the distance price ordinarily travels against an occurrence that still reaches its target. If your floor table for that block reads 32 ticks, a 20-tick stop sits inside the back-and-forth that most of your good occurrences go through first. A rule built that way gets stopped, then watches the move it expected happen without it. Run it through a test and the result describes where the stop sat. It says nothing about the fade.

Second Chance Entry
A popular name for re-entering at the same place after a first attempt is stopped out. In this course it is not a setup: a re-entry is the same setup plus a fresh trigger, and a written rule for re-entries is a new register entry.

A stop inside the noise is also where the urge for a second chance entry comes from: stopped, and then the move came. In this course a re-entry is the same setup plus a fresh trigger at a named level, with no count cap and the 2R kill switch as the limit (Module 02). A rule that re-enters at the same price because the first stop was too close is the noise problem, run twice.

The rule: before any rule is run, its stop is written by structure, behind the far edge of a named zone, or at your floor for that block, whichever is wider. A stop inside your own adverse-excursion record tests the stop, not the idea.

Getting it wrong looks like a run of small losses on mornings that went on to do exactly what the idea said, and a conclusion that the idea "needs tweaking". What it reveals: you are reading the stop's failure as the idea's, and every later change to the idea will be aimed at the wrong part. The fix: the report gets one extra count: trades stopped out where price then reached the target inside the rule's window. Write it beside the loss count. If that count is a large share of the losses, the reason line in the register says "stop inside the noise", and any new stop is a new entry, run from scratch.

Gap Fill To The Prior Close: A Different Event

Gap
An RTH open outside the prior day's range: above the PD High or below the PD Low. Nothing else is a gap in this course. The Gap Setups (Module 12) target the PD range edge, not the prior close.

Show it with the prior session from Module 12: PD range 21,520 to 21,690, prior close 21,671. The popular idea says: when the market opens away from yesterday's close, trade from the open until the gap fills back to that close. Its "gap" is measured from the close. So an open at 21,682, inside yesterday's range, is a gap up to that idea. In this course it is not a gap at all.

That one difference changes the event. The idea's sessions include every open inside yesterday's range that sits away from the close, which are sessions this course never calls gaps, and any rate quoted for it counts those sessions. It is a number about a different set of mornings from the ones on your gap line. This page quotes no fill rate, from anyone, for exactly that reason. And "from the open" has a second problem the next part covers: the open is where the first RTH trade printed, not a location, so the idea has no entry, only a target.

The rule: before a popular idea goes into your register, its condition and its target are rewritten in this course's definitions. If the rewrite changes which sessions qualify, it is a new idea, and no number attached to the original comes with it. A gap branch on your plan aims at the PD range edge, never the prior close.

Getting it wrong looks like a register entry that says "gap fill" with a fill rate copied beside it, or a plan whose target slot says "yesterday's close". What it reveals: you are borrowing a number from a different event and attaching it to the one on your chart, so the entry was answered before it was asked. The fix: a translation line at the top of every register entry that started as someone else's idea: "Original idea: … In course terms: … Sessions it covers: …". If the last line differs from the original's, the entry's number fields start blank and stay blank until your own test fills them.

A Touch Rate Treated As An Edge: A Magnet Taken For An Entry

Touch Rate
The share of sessions in which price reached a line at any point in a stated window, counted from a stated start such as the open. It says nothing about the order of events, where an entry sat, or whether a stop was hit first.

Show it with an invented line. A level is reached on most sessions of some sample, and the idea says: since price gets there most days, be in a trade toward it. On a composite morning the line is 40 points above price, and price is mid-range, with the nearest structure for a stop 50 points below. The line may well be reached today. The trade can still lose, because the stop can be taken first and the line reached afterwards, without you.

Module 12 makes the same point: a line reached often can still lose money from a badly placed entry. Module 21 shows the arithmetic in full. The test exposes the magnet by refusing to score the line. It scores a trade: an entry at a named location, a stop behind structure, the line as target, and which of stop and target price reached first. Move the entry and the result changes completely while the touch rate stays exactly where it was.

Expectancy
The average result per trade in R: win % × average win − loss % × average loss. It is the one number that combines how often a rule wins with how much it wins and loses.

The rule: an idea built on a touch rate is run as a full trade, with entry, stop and target counted in the order price reached them, and reported as expectancy in R. The touch rate is never the result; at most it is a target input.

Getting it wrong looks like an entry taken from mid-range because "it gets there most days", or a stop widened because the target feels likely. What it reveals: the line's frequency is excusing the entry's location, which is the one thing the frequency never measured. The fix: the report template has no touch column. It has a target-before-stop column, from a named location, beside the expectancy. An idea that can only be described by its touch rate has no entry yet and goes to the parking lot, not the register.

Calling The Day From The Open: A Rule That Cannot Be Locked

Open Type
A name for how price behaved in the first minutes after the 09:30 open. It describes those minutes only. It is vocabulary, never a call for the rest of the day.

Show it on a composite morning. From 09:30 to 09:40 price runs up from the open without trading back to it. The idea says: open drive, so trend day, so hold longs all session. Try to write that as a locked paragraph and it stops at the first line. What is an open drive, in prices and minutes? What is a trend day, and when is it known? A trend day is a name for what a session turned out to be. It is only known at the close, and a rule that needs it at 09:40 is using information it could not have had.

Lookahead
Using, in a rule or a test, information that was not available at the moment the order would have gone in, such as a bar's high for an entry inside that bar, or a day's type for a trade in its first hour. A rule with lookahead cannot be traded as written.

The test exposes this before any data is opened, at the lock. A paragraph someone else could code without asking a question needs a numeric definition of the opening behaviour, checkable at a stated time, and a numeric definition of the day type, checked at the close. Even then the call is only half a test. Some day types happen often in any sample, so a call is right on a share of sessions by chance alone. The call's hit count means something only beside the count you would expect if the call were random: the share of sessions of that type in the same record, times the number of calls.

The rule: an idea whose condition is a label isn't a rule until every label in it has a numeric definition that can be checked at the moment of the order. Until then it is vocabulary, and your plan carries no day-type field (Module 05, Module 15).

Getting it wrong looks like remembering the mornings the call was right, or deciding at 15:00 that the morning's drive "was" a trend day. What it reveals: the label is scored by memory and after the fact, and memory keeps the hits. The fix: if you want to examine such an idea, it goes in the register, never the plan: the call written with its definition at the stated time, scored at 16:00 by the written definition, and the hit count kept beside the count expected by chance. A label with no numeric definition stays in the parking lot as a word.

The Filter Added After Looking: Data Mining

Data Mining
Running many versions of a rule (stops, windows, filters, years) on the same data and keeping the one that looked best. The kept version fits that data's accidents, and its result is not a test, because the data that chose it cannot also judge it.

This one isn't a popular idea so much as what happens to every idea, including your own. You run a rule. It comes back flat. You read the losers and notice many of them fell on Mondays, so you add "skip Mondays" and run it again. Better. You notice the worst trades came after a wide first bar, so you add a size limit on the first bar. Better again. Each filter was chosen by looking at the trades it removes, so of course it improves them. Try twenty filters on one sample and some of them will look good by chance alone. The same trap opens when the OR window, the stop or the date range is picked after a first look.

The test exposes data mining at the cuts. A filter fitted to one stretch of data has nothing to fit in the next, so its halves disagree, or its recent stretch fails, as soon as it meets sessions it was not chosen on. The worked case below shows that pattern from the other side: a rule whose halves disagree, and the filter that would hide it.

The rule: a filter, window or stop thought of after seeing results is a new rule with its own register entry, scored only on data it has never seen: data after the date it was written, or a later window held back from the start. It never edits the entry that suggested it.

This is the psychological part, and it is the one that does the most damage, because it feels like diligence. You like the idea. The flat result feels like a near miss, and each filter feels like understanding. But a rule rescued on the data that failed it has stopped being tested; it is being searched for. Getting it wrong looks like an entry whose rule has quietly grown three clauses since it was locked, or a result reported with no record of how many versions came before it. What it reveals: the register is measuring your attachment to the idea, not the idea. The fix: a tries count in every entry. Every version you run gets its own numbered row, so the number of tries is visible beside the result. A filter's entry carries the date it was thought of, and its data starts after that date. A result with no tries count is filed as an idea, not a result.

Every popular idea has a step a test would expose first: no null, a stop inside the noise, a different event, a magnet taken for an entry, a rule that cannot be locked, or a filter found in the data. Name the step before you run the test, and let your own record, never anyone's report, give the answer.

Worked Case: One Invented Idea, Failing At The Halves

Illustration · not a trade record

An invented register entry, built to show the steps in order and where one of them stops the idea. The rule was made up for this page. It is not a rule, setup or trigger from this course, it is not any of the popular ideas above, it uses no level, and every number below is invented and round enough to check by hand. It belongs to no one.

Lock. Entry 14: the Wide-Bar Fade rule (invented). Written and dated before any data was opened: "MNQ, 5-minute bars built from 1-minute data. Between 10:30 and 12:00 ET, when a 5-minute bar's range is at least twice the average range of the six 5-minute bars before it and it closes in the top quarter of its range, go short at the open of the next 1-minute bar after it closes; the mirror, long, for a close in the bottom quarter. Stop: 1 tick beyond that bar's extreme. Target: the bar's midpoint. One trade per session. Any trade still open at 12:30 ET is scored at that price. Data: January 2019 to June 2026. Costs: one round-turn commission and one tick of slippage per side, in R. Null: the same rule on 1-minute returns shuffled within 30-minute blocks, 200 shuffles, averaged. Cuts: halves by trade count, each calendar year, 2023–2026." Every condition is known when the wide bar closes, and the entry is the next bar's open, so there is no lookahead. The lock holds.

Null. n 220 trades. 112 winners: 51%. Average win +0.9R, average loss −0.77R. Expectancy: 0.51 × 0.9 − 0.49 × 0.77 = 0.459 − 0.377 = +0.08R. Null: 48% winners, average win +0.85R, average loss −0.77R, so 0.48 × 0.85 − 0.52 × 0.77 = 0.408 − 0.400 = +0.01R. The rule beats the null by 0.07R across the whole sample. This step holds.

Halves. Trades 1 to 110 (January 2019 to mid-2022): 62 winners, 56%, +0.24R, null +0.01R. Trades 111 to 220 (mid-2022 to June 2026): 50 winners, 45%, −0.08R, null +0.01R. (Check: the two equal halves average (0.24 − 0.08) ÷ 2 = +0.08R, the whole sample's figure.) Opposite signs. This is the step it fails. The pooled +0.08R described neither half: it was one good stretch and one losing one, averaged.

Years. The rows show where the halves broke. 2019 to 2021 carry the sample; 2022 is barely above its null; every year from 2023 is below zero and below the null. The report shows every row, failing ones included, because the paragraph named the cut before the data was opened.

Recency, 2023–2026. n 100, 45 winners, 45%, −0.07R, null +0.01R. The stretch closest to the market you would trade is the one where the rule loses.

Gate 1: n of 220 is 100 or more, and the whole sample beats the volatility-preserving null, +0.08R against +0.01R. But Gate 1 also needs the rule positive in both halves and in every calendar year, and the second half and every year from 2023 are below zero. Not met, on this invented data. Gate 2 is never reached.

Label: the one your register gives a rule that failed its test (Module 22). Reason: "Failed at the halves: +0.24R, then −0.08R. Every year from 2023 below zero and below the null; recent stretch −0.07R." Reopen condition: "None for this paragraph. A different wide-bar rule, with a written reason to expect a different result, is a new entry, locked before it sees any data and scored on sessions after its lock date. A filter that removes the losing years, or the losing trades inside them, is not a reopen."

Hypothetical illustration. Hypothetical results have inherent limits: they are not actual trading and do not reflect real fills, slippage or the pressure of risk, and no representation is made that any account will achieve similar results.

Notice what the entry did next, which is nothing. The moment the halves came back, there were easy rescues on the table: skip trades after a wide first bar, skip 2023 as an unusual year, trade only Tuesdays, which happened to be positive. Each one would have lifted the number, and each one would have been chosen by reading the trades it removes. That is the last part of this page in action. The reopen condition was written while there was still no reason to want the rule back, and it says in advance that none of those counts.

Which Failure Is Your Idea Carrying?

Pick one idea you hold, your own or one you picked up. Answer from what is written down, not from what you believe about it. Score one point for each line that is true.

  1. Lockable. Every condition in it has a numeric definition that can be checked at the moment the order would go in: no label, no lookahead.
  2. Same event. Its condition and target are written in this course's definitions, so it counts the sessions on your own chart, and no number from anyone else's version is attached to it.
  3. Null. Its result will be read beside the same rule run on block-shuffled data, not beside zero or 50%.
  4. Outside the noise. Its stop is behind the far edge of a named zone or at your own floor for that block, whichever is wider.
  5. An entry, not a magnet. It is scored as a full trade from a named location, target before stop, in R, never by how often a line is touched.
  6. Clean. Every filter, window and stop in it was written before the data it will be scored on, and its tries count is recorded.

Line 1 overrides the score. If it is false, the idea is not a rule yet, however the rest scores: it stays in your parking lot as vocabulary until every label in it has a number. 6 of 6: write its locked paragraph and run it, in that order. 4 or 5: the false lines are fixed in the paragraph before any data is opened; a line you can't fix is the reason it fails, written down now. Under 4: it is a story, not an idea; it goes to the parking lot with today's date and the failure modes it carries beside it.

Before Your Next Session

Apply This Now

Take one trading idea you have heard or read this month. Write one line in your parking lot: the idea, in a few words, and the one failure mode from this page it is most exposed to. Do not open a chart for it. That's the whole assignment.

Where It Goes Next

This is the last module, and the test on it doesn't end with the course. Every idea you meet from here on goes the same way: into the parking lot of your PlayBook first (Module 23, Building Your Own PlayBook), and out of it only at a scheduled review, into your register as a locked, dated entry. The failure modes on this page are the words for that entry's reason line, and the entry's reopen condition is written the day it fails.

The test itself, the lock, the null, the cuts, the report and the two gates, is in Module 22, How To Test An Edge. Any number an idea arrives with is read by Module 21, Reading A Probability, before it goes anywhere near a plan. And the live record that answers the second gate is kept in Module 18, Journaling & Review, where the weekly review reads it at the display floor, on a fixed day, never called by a result.

MODULE 09 The Blueprint

Idea Generation

Generating an idea is not deciding which way the market will go; it is deciding which of your levels can matter today, how much room the day is likely to have, and which price would tell you that you were wrong, all before the first bar of the session prints.

The Morning You Already Knew The Direction

You know the morning. CPI came in below the forecast, the overnight jumped on the print, someone in a chat room you follow wrote "trend day up", and the daily RSI was high enough that another account called the market stretched. By 09:20 you had four opinions about the session and no price at which any of them would be wrong. At 09:47 you bought a dip in the middle of the range because the number was good. At 10:05 you sold a push because the RSI was high. Both stopped out, and neither trade came from a level.

None of those inputs was useless. Each one was used for the wrong job. A release tells you when activity arrives, not which way it goes. The calendar tells you what kind of day it may be for activity, not for price. ATR tells you how far a session usually travels, not where to. RSI tells you how one-sided the last two weeks were, and nothing about tomorrow. And another trader's call tells you about their system, which you can't see.

This page teaches the first tier of the chain, the idea, built from those inputs into something a price can prove wrong. Six parts, mechanical first: catalysts, the calendar, ATR and RSI, the idea itself, the two reads you grade apart, and last, the one about you: whose read it is.

Catalysts Set The Clock, Not The Direction

Every trade in this course traces one chain: idea → setup (location + read) → trigger → construction (level → stop → risk → size → entry). The idea comes first, and it is the only tier that is written before the session. Its raw material is the day's macro news, its scheduled releases and what the overnight already did with them.

Catalyst
An event that brings participants to the market at a known or sudden time, such as a Tier One release, a Fed decision or an unscheduled headline. In idea generation it sets the clock and can print new levels; it never sets a direction.

Show it. At 08:30 CPI prints below the forecast. At 08:31 MNQ trades 50 points higher and prints the overnight high there. By 09:05 price has given back most of that and sits back inside yesterday's range. The release did three things you can write down: it arrived at a known time, it brought a burst of activity, and it printed a level, the overnight high on the spike. It did not tell you the session's direction. The reaction carries more information than the number, and the reaction is read where it happens: at your levels, after the session opens.

Macro works the same way on a longer clock. A theme the market is trading this month, rates or earnings or a policy fight, explains why participants are active and which releases they care about. It does not choose a side for a 09:30 to 11:00 branch. Neither does another market: yields, the dollar, oil or the other index are never a lead for a branch. The idea is written on your instrument's chart, at your instrument's levels.

Tier One releases (CPI, PPI, PCE, NFP, FOMC with the press conference, Retail Sales) carry the timing rule from Module 02: flat five minutes before, no new entries until two minutes after. That rule exists because the seconds around a release are the least readable of the day, not because the release has a direction you are waiting to learn.

The rule: a catalyst goes on the sheet as an event and a time, with T−5 and T+2 for a Tier One release, plus any level it printed. The surprise, above or below the forecast, is not written on the sheet and never sets the direction of a branch.

The figure behind that rule: across 72 NFP releases from June 2020 to May 2026, the direction of the surprise matched the direction of NQ price in 33 of the 70 releases scored (TradingStats, app data, n=72). That is close to a coin flip in either direction. It is a base rate from one closed data set (Module 21), and it says one thing: knowing which way the number surprised did not tell you which way price went.

Getting it wrong looks like "CPI cool, longs only today", or a short taken at 08:33 because the number was hot. What it reveals: a number someone else published chose your side before any level was tested, so the session is spent waiting for the market to agree with the headline. The fix: the catalyst line on the sheet accepts four things only: the event, its time, T−5 and T+2, and the level it printed, if any. Any word about the number itself (hot, cool, beat, miss) is struck through before 09:25.

The Calendar Sets Activity, Not Price

Some days are different for reasons printed on a calendar months ahead. Forced orders bunch into known windows: options expire, index futures expire, funds rebalance at month-end (Module 04). These belong to the idea as context about range and activity.

OpEx (Options Expiration)
The day each month when listed equity and index options expire. On the pre-market sheet it is written as a noun on the calendar line: context about activity for the session, never a direction.
Quad Witching
The name traders give to the quarterly expiry Friday in March, June, September and December, when the quarterly equity index futures expire (Module 03) on the same day as that month's options. Written on the calendar line as activity context, never as a direction.
Turn Of Month
The last few sessions of one month and the first few of the next, when month-end rebalancing and new-month flows are scheduled. Written on the calendar line as activity context, never as a direction.

The dates are fixed by the contracts. Standard monthly SPX options expire on the third Friday of the expiration month (Cboe, SPX options specifications), and the quarterly index futures expire on that same Friday in March, June, September and December (Module 03).

Show it. A calendar line that passes: "Turn of month: month-end close tomorrow. Not expiry week. Not roll week. Tier One: CPI 08:30." Every item is a noun and a date. A Tier One day goes on the same line, because a release is the sharpest calendar event of all.

What the calendar line changes is your expectation of activity, and through it, how you read the room in the next section: a quad witching Friday or a month-end close is a day when forced orders arrive in known windows, so a busy close comes as no surprise. What it never changes is which side a branch takes. You will read claims that some calendar days tend to rise or fall. No figure about how these days move appears on your sheet or on this page. Calendar tendencies quoted elsewhere are mostly daily stock-index statistics, many disagree with each other from one sample to the next, and none is a measurement of your trades on your instrument in your window.

The rule: calendar items (OpEx, quad witching, turn of month, Tier One days, roll week) are written as nouns on the calendar line. They set expectations of range and activity. They never set a direction, and no figure about how such days tend to move goes on the sheet.

Getting it wrong looks like "OpEx Friday, it'll pin, fade every move", or "month-end, funds have to buy". What it reveals: a timetable for activity has been turned into a forecast, and the forecast is now choosing trades the levels never offered. The fix: the calendar line accepts only nouns and dates; any up, down, long, short, pin or "should" on it is struck through before 09:25. And on each trade's journal entry, copy the calendar line's nouns into the notes, so that after enough trades your own record, not someone's chart of averages, can show whether your trades behave differently on those days.

ATR Sets The Room; RSI Sets Nothing

Two indicators sit on most charts. One of them answers a question the idea needs. The other answers a question the idea never asks.

ATR (Average True Range)
The average of the true range over a set number of bars, commonly 14. True range is the largest of: the bar's high minus its low, the high minus the prior close, and the prior close minus the low. It describes how far price usually travels in a bar; it says nothing about direction.
RSI (Relative Strength Index)
An oscillator from 0 to 100 that compares the size of recent up closes with recent down closes over a set number of bars, commonly 14. It describes how one-sided the recent run has been. In this course it is never a thesis, a trigger, an exit or a direction.

Show ATR at work. The 14-day ATR of the RTH session is 310 points. The overnight range is 130 points, from 21,880 to 22,010. A session of ordinary size, measured from the overnight low, would reach about 22,190; measured from the overnight high, about 21,700. Those two prices are the room frame: an ordinary day's travel in either direction from where the market already is. A level inside the frame is one price can plausibly reach today. A target beyond it asks for an unusual day. ATR will come back in Module 14, where it sizes the stop distance against structure; here it only frames the day.

Now RSI. The daily RSI reads 74. That says the last fourteen sessions closed up more, and by more, than they closed down. It is a description of the past. It doesn't say the move is spent, and it doesn't say it will continue: a high reading can stay high through a long advance, and a low one through a long decline. So it has no slot on the sheet. The same goes for a run of narrow days: a quiet week is not an input, and it promises nothing about tomorrow's range.

The rule: ATR goes on the sheet as one number in points and the two room-frame prices. No other indicator reading goes on the sheet, and no indicator sets a thesis, a trigger, an exit or a direction.

Getting it wrong looks like "RSI 74, overbought, look for shorts", or a branch whose target is two ATRs away on an ordinary morning. What it reveals: a description of the past is being read as a forecast, or the plan is asking the day for more than days usually give, so the trade will be held past its sense waiting for it. The fix: the sheet has one indicator line, written as "ATR [points] · frame [low price] to [high price]". Any other indicator value written anywhere on the sheet is struck through, and any target outside the frame is marked on the branch as "outside room".

The Idea: Thesis, Invalidation, Conviction

Now the inputs become an idea. Three parts, always in this order.

Idea
A conditional statement written before the session: a thesis in if/then form at a named level, the invalidation that ends it, and a conviction grade of strong, moderate or thin. It describes what you will do if price reaches a place, not where price will go.
Thesis
The if/then part of an idea: a named behaviour at a named level zone, the trigger that must fire there, and the level it targets. It states what you will do if price does something at a place, never where price will go.
Invalidation
The named price, judged on a candle close of a stated timeframe, at which a branch's thesis is wrong. It sits at or behind the zone's far edge. Written before the session, never moved during it.
Conviction
A label of strong, moderate or thin attached to one branch: how much structure and context stand behind its thesis. It is recorded for the journal. It doesn't change your size and it isn't a direction.

A branch is one conditional line of the plan, and a plan usually holds one on each side of price plus a line where you do nothing (Module 15 teaches the full plan). The inputs from the last three sections enter a branch in exactly three places. The context and the catalyst decide which levels are worth a branch at all. The room frame decides whether the target is reachable on an ordinary day. The clock decides whether a Tier One release lands inside your window. Everything else on the chart is the level and the behaviour at it.

Conviction is counted, not felt. Three checks, one point each:

  1. Zone. The zone holds more than one level from your list, such as the pd-high and the on-high within a few points of each other.
  2. Path. No other level from your list sits between the zone and the target.
  3. Room and clock. The target sits inside the ATR room frame, and no Tier One release falls inside the 09:30 to 11:00 window.

Three points is strong, two is moderate, one or none is thin. The reason clause names the check that failed: "moderate: pd-low sits in the path". This page uses three checks so the label is written the same way every morning; whether your strong branches behave differently from your thin ones is something only your own record can show, once there are enough of each. Conviction never touches size. Size comes from the grade, in one table, in Module 14.

The rule: every idea is a thesis at a named level zone, an invalidation price with its candle timeframe, and a conviction counted from the three checks with a one-clause reason. An idea without a named invalidation is No Setup.

Getting it wrong looks like "looking for longs above yesterday's high, feels strong". What it reveals: the inputs were turned into a mood rather than a branch, so there is no price at which the idea ends and no record of why you believed it. The fix: write each branch backwards: invalidation first, then the zone and trigger, then the target, then the three checks, ticked one by one, and the label last. A branch with no invalidation line is crossed out and the space goes to the nothing case.

Two Reads: The Market And Your Execution

An idea can be right and the trade still lose. A release spike can take the stop on a branch whose level later held exactly as written. And a trade can win off an idea that was wrong, because you entered somewhere the plan never named. If the result grades both at once, you learn nothing from either.

Market Read
Your pre-session judgment of where the day's business can happen: the context, the levels that get branches, the room and the clock. After the session it is graded on one question: did price do at the level what a branch said it might, or did the nothing case hold?
Execution Read
How you acted on the plan: whether the trigger fired before entry, the stop sat behind structure, the size matched the plan, and the exit followed it. It is graded by the grade rubric and the execution mark, never by the result.

Show it. A branch said: if price accepts above the pd-high zone and a Pause N Go fires, target the prior-week high. Price accepted, paused, and went. You entered early, before the pause resolved, and the first wobble stopped you out. Market read: right. Execution read: the trigger hadn't fired, so the grade rubric (Module 14) marks the entry down. The trade lost 1R. The loss was an execution leak, not a read leak, and the fix lives in the trigger, not in the idea.

The reverse matters as much. A trade from the middle of the range that happened to win is a market read the plan never made and an execution with no level. Its result says nothing good about either.

The rule: after every session, the market read and the execution read are graded on separate lines, and neither is graded by the trade's result.

Getting it wrong looks like rewriting the idea process after a losing day when every branch played out as written, or keeping a habit because it won. What it reveals: the result is grading everything, so the part that worked gets changed and the part that leaked gets kept. The fix: two lines in the evening review, written before you look at the day's R. "Read: right / wrong / not reached" for each branch and the nothing case. "Execution: [grade] · [execution mark]" for each trade. Only then write the R. A day can be "read right, execution B" and be the most useful day of the week.

Your Read, Not Theirs

The last input is the one that arrives without being asked for: other people's reads. A desk's morning note, a chat room's "trend day up", a feed full of confident calls. Each one sounds like information, and the pull to take one is strongest on exactly the mornings when your own sheet looks uncertain.

Show what a borrowed call contains. "Trend day up" names no level, no behaviour, no trigger, no invalidation and no conviction you can count. You don't know the system it came from, the timeframe it is meant for or where its author gets out. It is a label for a whole session, and no price at 09:45 can prove it wrong. If you trade it and it fails, you can't grade it, because the reasons were never yours. If you trade it and it works, you have learned to need the next one.

This is the hard part, and it isn't about information. A confident voice offers what the session never will: an answer before it starts. Taking it feels like preparation. It is outsourcing the one tier of the chain that is yours to build, and it moves your conviction from what you can point at on the chart to who agrees with you.

The rule: another trader's day-type call or direction is a label, not an input. Nothing goes on the sheet that you did not build from your own levels, room and clock. If an outside idea can't be rebuilt as your own branch, with a slug, a trigger and an invalidation price, it isn't on the sheet.

Getting it wrong looks like opening the chat before the sheet, moving a branch from thin to strong because a respected account said the same thing, or adding a branch at 09:24 that matches a post. What it reveals: conviction has become social, so the idea will be held for as long as the voice stays confident rather than until its invalidation prints. The fix: the sheet is written before any outside source is opened; chats, feeds and notes stay closed until 09:25. The sheet has no field for anyone else's view. Conviction comes only from the three checks; a reason clause that names a person or a post is struck through.

An idea is your levels, your room and your clock turned into branches a price can prove wrong. Catalysts, the calendar and indicators decide which levels matter today and how much room the day has; none of them, and no one else's call, decides the direction.

Worked Case: One Morning, Built Into An Idea

Illustration · not a trade record

A composite MNQ morning, built to show the inputs turning into an idea. It belongs to no one, the prices are invented for the arithmetic, and the case stops at 09:25, before the open. Points are index points on the MNQ chart.

Catalyst, 09:00. CPI at 08:30 (Tier One): T−5 08:25, T+2 08:32, already past. The release printed the overnight high, 22,010, at 08:31. The number came in below the forecast; that sentence is not written on the sheet. No other Tier One release today, so none falls inside the 09:30 to 11:00 window.

Calendar line. "Turn of month: month-end close tomorrow. Not expiry week. Not roll week. Tier One: CPI 08:30." Nouns and dates only. It tells you tomorrow's close may be busy; it tells you nothing about today's direction.

Context at 09:05. Price 21,968. Against the PD range (21,840 to 21,995): inside. Against PD value (21,872 to 21,960): above. ON range 21,880 to 22,010: its high is above the PD range, printed on the release.

Room. "ATR 310 · frame 21,700 to 22,190." That is 21,880 + 310 and 22,010 − 310, an ordinary day's travel from each end of the range already printed.

Struck from the sheet. "Daily RSI 74" (an indicator reading with no slot). "Chat, 08:50: trend day up, CPI cool" (a borrowed label; the chat was opened before the sheet, which is itself the mistake from the last section, and it is noted in the review).

Levels (price 21,968; above price the far edge is the top, below it the bottom):

Which levels get a branch. All of them sit inside the room frame, so the frame removes none. The two zones that hold two levels each, pd-high · on-high above and on-low · pd-val below, are the two edges of the range the release already printed. They get branches. The pd-vah and pd-vpoc in between go to the nothing case.

Branch 1, written backwards. Invalidation: a 5-minute close back below 21,989. Level and trigger: if price accepts above the pd-high · on-high zone (21,989 to 22,010), meaning a 5-minute close above 22,010 within 15 minutes of the first touch, and a Pause N Go fires on the first pullback into the zone. Target: prior-week-high, 22,120. Checks: zone, two levels, yes; path, no level between 22,010 and 22,120, yes; room and clock, 22,120 is inside the frame's 22,190 and no Tier One falls in the window, yes. Conviction: strong, three of three.

Branch 2. Invalidation: a 5-minute close back above 21,886. Level and trigger: if price accepts below the on-low · pd-val zone (21,872 to 21,886), meaning a 5-minute close below 21,872 within 15 minutes of the first touch, and a Pause N Go fires on the first pullback into the zone. Target: htf-lvn, 21,786. Checks: zone, two levels, yes; path, the pd-low zone sits between, no; room and clock, 21,786 is inside the frame's 21,700 and no Tier One in the window, yes. Conviction: moderate: pd-low sits in the path.

Nothing case: between 21,886 and 21,989, without acceptance outside it: no trade. That includes the pd-vah and the pd-vpoc. A first touch of either zone that isn't accepted within 15 minutes: no trade. A gap open, above 21,995 or below 21,840: no gap branch is written, so no trade.

Final read, as it is said aloud in the rehearsal:

"Inside the PD range, above PD value, overnight high printed on CPI, turn of month, no Tier One in the window, room 21,700 to 22,190. If price accepts above 22,010 and a Pause N Go fires on the pullback, long toward the prior-week high at 22,120, wrong on a 5-minute close below 21,989, strong. If price accepts below 21,872 and a Pause N Go fires on the pullback, short toward the htf-lvn at 21,786, wrong on a 5-minute close above 21,886, moderate, pd-low in the path. Between 21,886 and 21,989, and on a gap open, nothing."

Four sentences. The CPI number, the RSI and the chat call are nowhere in it. The stop, the size and the entry come from Module 14 at the trigger, and whether the long branch's 110 points to the target clear 2:1 against the stop the structure needs is checked there, not here. The idea ends at 09:25; what happens after 09:30 belongs to Module 17.

Notice what the inputs did. The release supplied a time and a level. The calendar supplied a noun. ATR supplied two prices that checked the targets. None of them picked a side: both branches are written, one strong and one moderate, and the widest line on the sheet is still the one that says "no trade".

Whose Idea Is It?

Take your last five pre-market sheets. Score each one, a point per line that is true:

  1. Catalyst: each release written as event, time, T−5 and T+2, with no word about the number.
  2. Calendar: nouns and dates only, no direction word.
  3. Room: ATR in points and the two frame prices; no other indicator value anywhere on the sheet.
  4. Thesis: every branch names a slug zone, a behaviour, a trigger and a target level.
  5. Invalidation: every branch has a price with its candle timeframe.
  6. Conviction: every branch carries strong, moderate or thin, counted from the three checks, with a reason clause naming the failed check.
  7. Your own: no day-type label, no direction call and nothing from another trader's view appears anywhere on the sheet.

7 of 7 is an idea. The threshold: if any sheet missed line 5, trades taken from it were No Setup, and the invalidation line is the first thing to fix. If line 7 failed on any of the five, your next five sheets are written with every chat, feed and note closed until 09:25. If two or more sheets scored under 7 for any other reason, find the line you miss most often and fix that one before adding anything else to the sheet.

Before Your Next Session

Apply This Now

Tomorrow morning, before you open anyone else's view, write one line: the day's catalysts with their times, the calendar line in nouns, and "ATR [points] · frame [low] to [high]". One line. That's the whole assignment.

Further reading

Reading List

Books that sit behind the habits this course teaches: thinking in probabilities, reviewing your own work and keeping risk fixed. Each has one line on why it earns a place on your shelf.

All tools

The titles come from seven published recommendation lists, listed at the bottom. A book is not a lesson in this course, and none of it is trading advice. Read for the ideas and test them against your own journal.

Named on two or more lists

These titles show up on more than one of the seven lists we read, so they come first.

  • Trading in the Zone, Mark Douglas. Treats trading as a skill of thinking in probabilities, so one outcome stops steering the next decision.
  • Thinking, Fast and Slow, Daniel Kahneman. Explains the two modes of thought behind snap calls and deliberate ones, and where each goes wrong under pressure.
  • The Psychology of Trading, Brett Steenbarger. Looks at the habits and emotional patterns that shape how a trader executes, from a working psychologist's view.
  • Enhancing Trader Performance, Brett Steenbarger. Frames improvement as deliberate practice and review, the same loop your journal runs.
  • The Daily Trading Coach, Brett Steenbarger. Short daily exercises for building self-awareness and routine around the work of trading.
  • Trading to Win, Ari Kiev. A performance-psychology take on goals, focus and following through on a plan.
  • Reminiscences of a Stock Operator, Edwin Lefevre. A fictionalized account of a speculator's career that shows how timeless the temptations of the tape are.
  • Nudge, Richard H. Thaler and Cass Sunstein. Shows how the way a choice is set up changes what people pick, which is useful when you design your own checklist.
  • Predictably Irrational, Dan Ariely. Experiments showing that our errors in judgment follow patterns, so they can be planned around.
  • Why Smart People Make Big Money Mistakes, Gary Belsky and Thomas Gilovich. Catalogs the mental traps that cost informed people money and what to do about each.
  • Your Money and Your Brain, Jason Zweig. Connects brain science to the way people react to gains, losses and uncertainty.

Psychology

How you think, react and recover while you trade.

  • Investing and the Irrational Mind, Robert Koppel. Applies cognitive-bias research to the decisions investors make.
  • The Genius in All of Us, David Shenk. Argues that skill comes from sustained practice more than fixed talent.
  • Mindset: The New Psychology of Success, Carol Dweck. Contrasts a fixed view of ability with a growth view, and what each does to how you handle mistakes.
  • The Investor's Quotient, Jake Bernstein. Applies psychology to the swings of crowd mood that move markets.
  • The Mental Edge in Trading, Jason Williams. Practical mental-skills work for focus and discipline while trading.
  • Trading Psychology 2.0, Brett Steenbarger. Updates the case for treating trading as a performance field, with methods for working on yourself.

Decision-making

How judgment works, where it breaks, and how to build around it.

  • Neuroeconomics: Decision Making and the Brain, Paul W. Glimcher et al (eds). An academic collection on how the brain weighs value and risk, for readers who want the science.
  • Behavioral Finance: Investors, Corporations, and Markets, H. Kent Baker and John R. Nofsinger. A broad academic survey of how psychology shows up in financial decisions.
  • The Psychology of Judgment and Decision Making, Scott Plous. A compact textbook on how judgment errors arise and how to spot them.
  • The Little Book of Behavioral Investing, James Montier. A short guide to the biases that trip investors and ways to guard against them.
  • Investor Behavior: The Psychology of Financial Planning and Investing, H. Kent Baker and Victor Ricciardi. A multi-author collection on behavior in investing and planning.
  • Behavioral Finance and Investor Types, Michael Pompian. Sorts investor temperaments and the biases each one is prone to.
  • The Psychology of Investing, John R. Nofsinger. An overview of how emotion and bias affect investing choices.
  • The Behavior Gap, Carl Richards. Shows how our own timing decisions, not the investment, drive the difference in what people end up with.
  • The Sharp Brains Guide to Brain Fitness, SharpBrains (Alvaro Fernandez and Elkhonon Goldberg). A general look at attention and cognitive training, for readers curious about the mental side.
  • The Misbehavior of Markets, Benoit Mandelbrot. Argues that price moves are wilder than simple models assume, which is why risk limits matter.
  • The Little Book of Safe Money, Jason Zweig. A short guide to protecting savings from common mistakes and sales pitches.
  • The Psychology of Money, Morgan Housel. Short essays on how behavior, more than knowledge, shapes outcomes with money.
  • Misbehaving: The Making of Behavioral Economics, Richard H. Thaler. A first-person history of how behavioral economics challenged standard assumptions.
  • The Black Swan, Nassim Nicholas Taleb. On rare, high-impact events and why you should size for what you cannot forecast.
  • Fooled By Randomness, Nassim Nicholas Taleb. Shows how easily luck is mistaken for skill, a useful warning for reading your own results.
  • When Genius Failed, Roger Lowenstein. The story of a fund that failed through leverage and overconfidence.
  • Against the Gods: The Remarkable Story of Risk, Peter Bernstein. A history of how people learned to measure and manage risk.

Trading process

Method, review and risk, written by people who trade or study trading.

  • Market Wizards (series), Jack Schwager. Interviews with traders about how they approach risk and process, to read for method rather than for results.
  • How to Make Money in Stocks, William O'Neil. A rules-driven stock-selection system, useful as an example of writing your method down.
  • The PlayBook, Mike Bellafiore. Shows how to build a catalog of named setups and review each one, the structure behind your own setup list.
  • Market In Profile, Jim Dalton. Teaches how to read the distribution of price and time, the background for value and acceptance.
  • High Probability ETF Trading, Larry Connors and Cesar Alvarez. Short-term ETF strategies written out as testable rules.
  • Trend Following, Michael Covel. Describes trend-following as a rules-based approach, with profiles of those who use it.
  • Trading for a Living, Alexander Elder. Covers psychology, method and risk management, with an emphasis on keeping records.
  • Schwager on Futures: Technical Analysis, Jack Schwager. A reference on chart tools and patterns as applied to futures.
  • The Art and Science of Technical Analysis, Adam Grimes. A disciplined look at market structure and the case for testing what you trade.
  • Evidence-Based Technical Analysis, David Aronson. Makes the case for treating chart rules as hypotheses to test, with attention to data-mining bias.
  • Option Volatility & Pricing, Sheldon Natenberg. A standard reference on how options are priced, for the later options track.
  • Options Trading: The Hidden Reality, Charles Cottle. A critical look at what options trading involves in practice.
  • Practical Speculation, Victor Niederhoffer. A speculator's view of probability and markets, with a skeptical streak.
  • The Alchemy of Finance, George Soros. A theory of how market participants' views feed back into prices.
  • Trader Vic: Methods of a Wall Street Master, Victor Sperandeo. One trader's account of method, risk and market observation.
  • Trader Vic II: Principles of Professional Speculation, Victor Sperandeo. A follow-up on principles for speculating with defined risk.
  • The Hedge Fund Edge, Mark Boucher. A look at how fund managers think about process and risk control.
  • Street Smarts: High Probability Short-Term Trading Strategies, Linda Raschke and Larry Connors. Short-term setups written as explicit entry and exit rules.
  • Analysis of Financial Time Series, Ruey Tsay. A technical text on statistical methods for price data, for readers who want the math.
  • One Good Trade, Mike Bellafiore. Shows how to review and improve one trade at a time, which is the habit behind a journal.

Investing

A short pointer for the later finance track, not the focus of this course. These are the long-term, low-cost investing titles from the lists.

  • The Little Book of Common Sense Investing, John C. Bogle. The case for low-cost index funds, in a short read.
  • A Random Walk Down Wall Street, Burton Malkiel. A classic argument for broad, low-cost investing and against forecasting.
  • The Four Pillars of Investing, William Bernstein. Covers theory, history, psychology and business of investing in one book.
  • The Intelligent Investor, Benjamin Graham. The long-standing text on margin of safety and temperament in investing.
  • Winning the Loser's Game, Charles Ellis. Argues that avoiding errors matters more than finding winners in investing.
  • All About Asset Allocation, Rick Ferri. Explains how to split a portfolio across asset classes and rebalance it.
  • The Bogleheads' Guide to Investing, Taylor Larimore and Michael LeBoeuf and Mel Lindauer. A broad primer on long-term, low-cost investing.
  • The Bogleheads' Guide to the Three-Fund Portfolio, Taylor Larimore. A simple index-fund layout explained step by step.
